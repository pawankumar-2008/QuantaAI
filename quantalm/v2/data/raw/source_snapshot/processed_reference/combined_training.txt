﻿ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

1. INTRODUCTION TO ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science concerned with creating systems that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence.

These tasks include:
- Learning from experience.
- Reasoning and problem-solving.
- Understanding language.
- Recognizing images and patterns.
- Making decisions.
- Planning and prediction.

Examples of AI applications include virtual assistants, recommendation systems, fraud detection, autonomous vehicles, and medical image analysis.

2. GOALS OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

The major goals of AI are:
- Develop systems that can learn from data.
- Enable machines to reason and solve problems.
- Understand and process human language.
- Recognize objects, speech, and patterns.
- Automate repetitive and complex tasks.
- Support intelligent decision-making.

3. HISTORY OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

1950:
Alan Turing proposed the imitation game, commonly known as the Turing Test.

1956:
The term Artificial Intelligence was introduced at the Dartmouth Conference.

1960s and 1970s:
Early AI research focused on symbolic reasoning, problem-solving, and expert systems.

1980s:
Expert systems became popular in commercial applications.

1990s:
Machine learning approaches gained importance.

2010s:
Deep learning made major progress in image recognition, speech recognition, and language processing.

2020s:
Generative AI and large language models became widely used for text, image, audio, and code generation.

4. TYPES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BASED ON CAPABILITY

Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI):
AI designed to perform specific tasks. Examples include spam filters, recommendation systems, and voice assistants.

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):
A hypothetical form of AI that can understand and perform a broad range of intellectual tasks at a human-like level.

Artificial Superintelligence (ASI):
A hypothetical form of intelligence that would significantly exceed human intellectual capabilities.

Modern deployed AI systems are generally examples of narrow AI.

5. TYPES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BASED ON FUNCTIONALITY

Reactive Machines:
Systems that respond to current inputs without storing experiences for future decisions.

Limited Memory AI:
Systems that use information from past observations or training to make decisions.

Theory of Mind AI:
A proposed AI capability involving understanding human emotions, beliefs, and intentions.

Self-Aware AI:
A hypothetical AI system with consciousness and self-awareness.

6. INTELLIGENT AGENTS

An intelligent agent is a system that perceives its environment through sensors and takes actions through actuators to achieve goals.

Agent components:
- Sensors: Collect information from the environment.
- Percepts: Information received by the agent.
- Decision mechanism: Determines what action to take.
- Actuators: Perform actions in the environment.

Agent types:
- Simple reflex agent.
- Model-based reflex agent.
- Goal-based agent.
- Utility-based agent.
- Learning agent.

A rational agent selects actions that maximize expected performance based on its available information and objectives.

7. PROBLEM-SOLVING IN AI

AI problem-solving involves finding a sequence of actions that transforms an initial state into a desired goal state.

Important components:
- Initial state.
- Goal state.
- Actions.
- Transition model.
- Path cost.

Search algorithms:

Breadth-First Search (BFS):
Explores nodes level by level. It is complete for finite branching factors and finds a shortest path when all step costs are equal.

Depth-First Search (DFS):
Explores one branch deeply before backtracking. It uses less memory in many cases but does not generally guarantee the shortest path.

Uniform Cost Search:
Expands the node with the lowest accumulated path cost. It is optimal under suitable non-negative cost assumptions.

Greedy Best-First Search:
Selects nodes using a heuristic estimate of distance to the goal. It can be fast but is not generally optimal.

A* Search:
Uses both accumulated path cost and estimated remaining cost.

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

Where:
g(n) = Cost from the initial state to node n.
h(n) = Estimated cost from node n to the goal.

A* is optimal under suitable conditions, such as an admissible heuristic and appropriate graph-search handling.

8. KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

Knowledge representation is the method of storing facts, concepts, and relationships so an AI system can reason with them.

Common techniques:
- Logical representation.
- Semantic networks.
- Frames.
- Production rules.
- Ontologies.

Example:
Rule: If a person has a fever and cough, they may have a respiratory infection.
This is a simplified rule and does not establish a medical diagnosis.

9. MACHINE LEARNING

Machine Learning (ML) is a subfield of AI that enables systems to learn patterns from data and improve their performance without being explicitly programmed with every rule.

A machine learning workflow:
1. Define the problem.
2. Collect data.
3. Clean and preprocess data.
4. Select relevant features.
5. Split data into training, validation, and test sets.
6. Select a model.
7. Train the model.
8. Evaluate performance.
9. Tune parameters.
10. Deploy and monitor the model.

10. TYPES OF MACHINE LEARNING

Supervised Learning:
The model learns from labelled training data containing input-output pairs.

Common tasks:
- Classification: Predicts categories.
- Regression: Predicts continuous numerical values.

Examples:
- Email spam detection.
- House price prediction.
- Student performance prediction.

Unsupervised Learning:
The model discovers patterns in unlabelled data.

Common tasks:
- Clustering.
- Dimensionality reduction.
- Association rule mining.

Examples:
- Customer segmentation.
- Grouping similar documents.

Semi-Supervised Learning:
Uses a small amount of labelled data together with a larger amount of unlabelled data.

Reinforcement Learning:
An agent learns by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties.

11. SUPERVISED LEARNING ALGORITHMS

Linear Regression:
Predicts a continuous value using a linear relationship between input features and the target.

Logistic Regression:
A classification algorithm that estimates class probabilities using a logistic function in binary classification.

Decision Tree:
Uses a tree-like structure of decisions to make predictions.

Random Forest:
Combines predictions from multiple decision trees to improve robustness and predictive performance.

K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN):
Predicts the class or value of a data point using nearby training examples.

Support Vector Machine (SVM):
Finds a decision boundary that separates classes with a large margin.

Naive Bayes:
Uses Bayes' theorem with a conditional independence assumption between features.

12. UNSUPERVISED LEARNING ALGORITHMS

K-Means Clustering:
Partitions data into K clusters by assigning each point to the nearest cluster centroid and updating the centroids.

Hierarchical Clustering:
Builds a hierarchy of clusters by repeatedly merging or splitting groups.

DBSCAN:
Groups closely packed data points and identifies noise using density-based rules.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA):
A dimensionality-reduction technique that transforms features into orthogonal components capturing decreasing amounts of variance.

13. REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a learning approach in which an agent interacts with an environment and learns actions that maximize cumulative reward.

Main components:
- Agent: Learner or decision-maker.
- Environment: The world in which the agent operates.
- State: Current situation.
- Action: Decision taken by the agent.
- Reward: Feedback received after an action.
- Policy: Strategy used to select actions.

The agent aims to maximize expected cumulative reward.

Important concepts:
- Exploration: Trying actions to discover their outcomes.
- Exploitation: Choosing actions based on current knowledge.
- Discount factor: Determines the importance of future rewards.

14. DEEP LEARNING

Deep Learning is a subfield of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn complex patterns from data.

Applications:
- Image recognition.
- Speech recognition.
- Language translation.
- Object detection.
- Text generation.
- Medical image analysis.

Deep learning generally requires substantial training data and computational resources.

15. ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORKS

An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is a computational model inspired by the interconnected structure of biological neurons.

Main components:
- Input layer: Receives input features.
- Hidden layers: Learn intermediate representations.
- Output layer: Produces predictions.

A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies an activation function.

z = w1x1 + w2x2 + ... + wnxn + b

Output = Activation(z)

Where:
- x represents input values.
- w represents weights.
- b represents bias.
- Activation introduces non-linearity.

16. ACTIVATION FUNCTIONS

Activation functions introduce non-linearity into neural networks.

Sigmoid:
Maps an input to a value between 0 and 1.

ReLU:
Returns zero for negative inputs and the input itself for positive inputs.

ReLU(x) = max(0, x)

Tanh:
Maps inputs to values between -1 and 1.

Softmax:
Converts a vector of values into a probability distribution.

17. FORWARD PROPAGATION

Forward propagation is the process of passing input data through a neural network to calculate its output.

Steps:
1. Input data enters the input layer.
2. Each layer calculates weighted sums and biases.
3. Activation functions are applied.
4. The result is passed to the next layer.
5. The output layer generates the prediction.

18. BACKPROPAGATION

Backpropagation is an algorithm used to calculate gradients of a neural network's loss function with respect to its parameters.

Steps:
1. Perform forward propagation.
2. Calculate prediction error using a loss function.
3. Compute gradients using the chain rule.
4. Propagate gradient information backward through the network.
5. Use an optimizer to update weights and biases.

Backpropagation computes gradients; an optimizer such as gradient descent or Adam uses them to update parameters.

19. LOSS FUNCTIONS

A loss function measures the difference between model predictions and actual target values.

Mean Squared Error (MSE):
Commonly used for regression.

MSE = (1/n) * sum((actual - predicted)^2)

Mean Absolute Error (MAE):
Calculates the average absolute prediction error.

Binary Cross-Entropy:
Commonly used for binary classification.

Categorical Cross-Entropy:
Commonly used for multi-class classification.

The training process aims to minimize the selected loss function.

20. OPTIMIZATION ALGORITHMS

Gradient Descent:
Updates model parameters in the direction opposite to the loss gradient.

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD):
Updates parameters using one training example or a small sample at a time.

Mini-Batch Gradient Descent:
Updates parameters using a small batch of training examples.

Adam:
An adaptive optimization algorithm that maintains moving averages of gradients and squared gradients.

Learning Rate:
Controls the size of parameter updates during training.

21. CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a neural network architecture commonly used for image and spatial data.

Main components:
- Convolutional layer: Extracts local patterns using filters.
- Activation layer: Introduces non-linearity.
- Pooling layer: Reduces spatial dimensions.
- Fully connected layer: Produces final predictions.

Applications:
- Image classification.
- Face recognition.
- Object detection.
- Medical image analysis.

22. RECURRENT NEURAL NETWORKS

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) is designed to process sequential data by maintaining information from previous time steps.

Applications:
- Text processing.
- Speech recognition.
- Time-series forecasting.

Limitations:
Traditional RNNs can struggle with long-term dependencies because of vanishing and exploding gradients.

23. LSTM AND GRU

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a type of recurrent neural network designed to learn long-term dependencies.

LSTM uses:
- Forget gate.
- Input gate.
- Output gate.
- Cell state.

Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is another recurrent architecture that uses update and reset gates to control information flow.

Both architectures help address limitations of traditional RNNs.

27. TRANSFORMER ARCHITECTURE

A Transformer is a neural network architecture based on attention mechanisms rather than recurrence as its main sequence-processing mechanism.

Major components:
- Token embeddings.
- Positional information.
- Self-attention.
- Feed-forward networks.
- Residual connections.
- Layer normalization.

Transformers can process sequence positions in parallel during training, subject to attention-mask and architectural constraints.

28. SELF-ATTENTION MECHANISM

Self-attention allows each token to calculate how much attention it should give to other tokens in a sequence.

Three important vectors:
- Query (Q).
- Key (K).
- Value (V).

Scaled dot-product attention:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k))V

Where d_k is the dimension of the key vectors.

Attention helps the model capture relationships between different positions in a sequence.

29. MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION

Multi-head attention performs multiple attention operations in parallel.

Each attention head can learn different relationships between tokens.

The outputs of the heads are combined and projected to produce the final representation.

Benefits:
- Captures different types of relationships.
- Learns multiple contextual representations.
- Improves the model's ability to represent sequence information.

30. LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a neural network trained on large amounts of text to learn language patterns and predict tokens.

Many modern LLMs use Transformer architectures.

Typical training stages:
- Data collection and preparation.
- Tokenization.
- Pretraining.
- Instruction tuning or other adaptation.
- Evaluation.
- Deployment.

An autoregressive language model predicts the next token based on previous tokens.

31. TOKENIZATION IN LANGUAGE MODELS

Tokenization converts text into tokens that a language model can process.

Common approaches:
- Word-level tokenization.
- Character-level tokenization.
- Subword tokenization.

Subword tokenization divides text into frequently occurring smaller units and can represent many unseen words using known pieces.

Examples of subword methods include Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece.

Special tokens may represent:
- Beginning of sequence.
- End of sequence.
- Padding.
- Unknown tokens.
- Instruction or role boundaries.

32. AUTOREGRESSIVE TEXT GENERATION

Autoregressive generation produces text one token at a time.

Process:
1. Receive a prompt.
2. Convert the prompt into token IDs.
3. Pass the tokens through the model.
4. Obtain next-token scores or logits.
5. Convert scores into a probability distribution.
6. Select the next token.
7. Append the token to the sequence.
8. Repeat until a stopping condition is reached.

33. TEXT GENERATION PARAMETERS

Temperature:
Controls the randomness of token selection.

Lower temperature generally produces more predictable output. Higher temperature generally produces more varied output.

Top-K Sampling:
Restricts token selection to the K highest-scoring candidates.

Top-P Sampling:
Selects from the smallest group of candidates whose cumulative probability reaches a specified threshold.

Repetition Penalty:
Reduces the likelihood of repeatedly generating the same tokens.

Maximum New Tokens:
Limits how many tokens can be generated after the prompt.

34. RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines information retrieval with language generation.

Typical workflow:
1. Receive a user question.
2. Search a knowledge source for relevant information.
3. Retrieve the most relevant documents or passages.
4. Add retrieved information to the model's prompt.
5. Generate an answer grounded in the retrieved context.
6. Present source references when available.

Benefits:
- Uses external or updated information.
- Can answer questions about private documents.
- Reduces dependence on information stored only in model parameters.

RAG does not automatically guarantee factual accuracy. Retrieval quality and generated answers must be evaluated.

35. VECTOR DATABASES

Typical uses:

40. OVERFITTING AND UNDERFITTING

Overfitting:
A model performs well on training data but poorly on unseen data because it has learned patterns that do not generalize.

Underfitting:
A model fails to learn important patterns even from its training data.

Methods to reduce overfitting:
- More representative training data.
- Data augmentation.
- Regularization.
- Dropout.
- Early stopping.
- Appropriate model complexity.

Separating these datasets helps estimate how well a model generalizes to unseen examples.

### DOMAIN: COMPUTER_SCIENCE

﻿ALGORITHMS

An algorithm is a finite sequence of well-defined instructions used to solve a computational problem.

An algorithm accepts zero or more inputs, processes them through a sequence of steps, and produces an output.

An algorithm should have clearly defined inputs, outputs, unambiguous instructions, and a finite number of steps.

Algorithm analysis evaluates the resources required by an algorithm, particularly execution time and memory usage.

Time complexity describes how the number of operations performed by an algorithm grows with input size.

Space complexity describes how the memory requirements of an algorithm grow with input size.

Best-case complexity describes the minimum resources required for an input of a particular size.

Average-case complexity describes the expected resources required over a distribution of possible inputs.

Worst-case complexity describes the maximum resources required for inputs of a particular size.

Asymptotic analysis studies algorithm behaviour as input size becomes large.

Big O notation represents an asymptotic upper bound on an algorithm's growth rate.

Big Omega notation represents an asymptotic lower bound on an algorithm's growth rate.

Big Theta notation represents a tight asymptotic bound when matching upper and lower bounds exist.

LINEAR SEARCH

Linear search examines elements one by one until the target is found or all elements have been checked.

Linear search works on both sorted and unsorted collections.

The best-case time complexity of linear search is O(1), when the target is the first element.

The worst-case time complexity of linear search is O(n), when the target is last or absent.

The auxiliary space complexity of iterative linear search is O(1).

BINARY SEARCH

Binary search finds an element in a sorted collection by repeatedly dividing the search interval into two halves.

Binary search compares the target with the middle element.

If the target equals the middle element, the search succeeds.

If the target is smaller than the middle element, binary search continues in the left half.

If the target is larger than the middle element, binary search continues in the right half.

Binary search requires sorted input and a collection that supports efficient middle-element access.

The time complexity of binary search is O(log n) in the worst case.

The iterative implementation of binary search uses O(1) auxiliary space.

SORTING ALGORITHMS

Sorting is the process of arranging elements according to a specified order, such as ascending or descending order.

Bubble sort repeatedly compares adjacent elements and swaps them if they are in the wrong order.

Bubble sort has O(n squared) average and worst-case time complexity. An optimized implementation can achieve O(n) best-case time when no swaps are needed.

Selection sort repeatedly selects the smallest remaining element and places it at the beginning of the unsorted portion.

Selection sort has O(n squared) best, average, and worst-case time complexity.

Insertion sort builds a sorted portion by inserting each new element into its correct position.

Insertion sort has O(n squared) average and worst-case time complexity and O(n) best-case time complexity on already sorted input.

Merge sort follows the divide and conquer approach. It divides an array into smaller parts, sorts them, and merges the sorted parts.

Merge sort has O(n log n) time complexity in the best, average, and worst cases.

A typical array implementation of merge sort requires O(n) auxiliary space.

Quick sort selects a pivot element and partitions the array so that smaller elements are placed before the pivot and larger elements after it.

Quick sort recursively sorts the partitions.

Quick sort has O(n log n) average-case time complexity and O(n squared) worst-case time complexity.

Heap sort uses a binary heap to repeatedly extract the maximum or minimum element and build a sorted sequence.

Heap sort has O(n log n) best, average, and worst-case time complexity.

The divide and conquer technique divides a problem into smaller subproblems, solves them, and combines their results.

Merge sort and quick sort are common examples of divide and conquer algorithms.

RECURSION AND BACKTRACKING

Recursion is a technique in which a function calls itself to solve smaller instances of a problem.

A base case defines the condition under which recursive calls stop.

A recursive case reduces the problem into a smaller subproblem.

The call stack stores information about active function calls, including their local variables and return addresses.

Excessive recursion can cause stack overflow when the call stack exceeds its available capacity.

Backtracking explores possible solutions and abandons a path when it cannot lead to a valid solution.

Backtracking is commonly used in maze solving, Sudoku, the N-Queens problem, and generating combinations.

GREEDY ALGORITHMS

A greedy algorithm makes a locally optimal choice at each step in the hope of constructing a globally optimal solution.

A greedy choice is an immediate decision made according to a selection rule.

Greedy algorithms are effective for problems that satisfy suitable properties such as the greedy-choice property and optimal substructure.

Activity selection is a greedy problem in which the goal is to select the maximum number of non-overlapping activities.

Fractional knapsack uses a greedy strategy based on value per unit weight. It allows taking fractions of items.

Greedy algorithms do not always produce optimal solutions for every optimization problem.

DYNAMIC PROGRAMMING

Dynamic programming solves problems by breaking them into overlapping subproblems and storing their results to avoid repeated computation.

Optimal substructure means an optimal solution can be constructed from optimal solutions to smaller subproblems.

Overlapping subproblems occur when the same smaller problems are solved repeatedly.

Memoization is a top-down dynamic programming technique that stores results of previously solved subproblems.

Tabulation is a bottom-up dynamic programming technique that fills a table of subproblem results.

The Fibonacci sequence is a common example used to demonstrate dynamic programming.

The 0/1 knapsack problem is a dynamic programming problem in which each item must either be selected completely or not selected.

Dynamic programming can reduce repeated computation, but it may require additional memory to store intermediate results.

GRAPH ALGORITHMS

Breadth First Search (BFS) explores graph vertices level by level using a queue.

BFS can find shortest paths in an unweighted graph when the graph is represented appropriately.

Depth First Search (DFS) explores a graph deeply before backtracking. It uses recursion or an explicit stack.

With an adjacency list, BFS and DFS have O(V + E) time complexity, where V is the number of vertices and E is the number of edges.

Dijkstra's algorithm finds shortest paths from a source vertex to other vertices in a weighted graph with non-negative edge weights.

Dijkstra's algorithm is not generally correct when negative-weight edges are present.

Bellman-Ford is a shortest-path algorithm that supports negative edge weights and can detect reachable negative-weight cycles.

A minimum spanning tree is a subset of edges that connects every vertex of a connected, undirected, weighted graph without cycles and with minimum total weight.

Kruskal's algorithm constructs a minimum spanning tree by sorting edges by weight and selecting edges that do not create cycles.

Prim's algorithm constructs a minimum spanning tree by repeatedly adding the minimum-weight edge that connects the current tree to a new vertex.

Topological sorting produces a linear ordering of vertices in a directed acyclic graph such that every directed edge points from an earlier vertex to a later vertex.

QUESTION: What is an algorithm?
ANSWER: An algorithm is a finite sequence of well-defined instructions used to solve a problem. It accepts inputs, performs operations, and produces outputs.

QUESTION: What is the difference between linear search and binary search?
ANSWER: Linear search examines elements one by one and works on unsorted data. Binary search repeatedly divides a sorted search interval into halves and has O(log n) worst-case time complexity.

QUESTION: Why does binary search require sorted data?
ANSWER: Binary search decides which half of the collection can be discarded by comparing the target with the middle element. This decision is valid only when the elements are arranged in sorted order.

QUESTION: What is the difference between merge sort and quick sort?
ANSWER: Merge sort divides the input into halves and merges sorted results. It has O(n log n) worst-case time complexity and typically requires O(n) auxiliary space for arrays. Quick sort partitions elements around a pivot and has O(n log n) average-case time complexity, but O(n squared) worst-case time complexity.

QUESTION: What is recursion?
ANSWER: Recursion is a programming technique in which a function calls itself to solve smaller versions of a problem. A base case stops further recursive calls.

QUESTION: What is backtracking?
ANSWER: Backtracking explores possible solution paths and abandons a path when it cannot produce a valid solution. It is useful for constraint-based problems such as Sudoku and N-Queens.

QUESTION: What is a greedy algorithm?
ANSWER: A greedy algorithm selects the locally best available choice at each step. It produces an optimal solution only when the problem has suitable mathematical properties.

QUESTION: What is dynamic programming?
ANSWER: Dynamic programming solves problems by storing results of overlapping subproblems so that they do not need to be computed repeatedly. It can use memoization or tabulation.

QUESTION: What is the difference between memoization and tabulation?
ANSWER: Memoization is a top-down technique that stores results when recursive subproblems are solved. Tabulation is a bottom-up technique that computes and stores results in an organized order.

QUESTION: What is Dijkstra's algorithm?
ANSWER: Dijkstra's algorithm computes shortest paths from a source vertex to other vertices in a weighted graph with non-negative edge weights.

QUESTION: What is a minimum spanning tree?
ANSWER: A minimum spanning tree is a set of edges connecting all vertices of a connected, undirected, weighted graph without cycles and with minimum total edge weight.

QUESTION: What is the difference between BFS and DFS?
ANSWER: BFS explores vertices level by level using a queue. DFS explores a path deeply before backtracking and uses a stack or recursion.

QUESTION: What are the three common asymptotic notations?
ANSWER: Big O represents an asymptotic upper bound, Big Omega represents an asymptotic lower bound, and Big Theta represents a tight asymptotic bound.

﻿COMPUTER NETWORKS

1. INTRODUCTION TO COMPUTER NETWORKS

A computer network is a collection of interconnected devices that communicate and share resources using communication links and protocols.

Objectives:
- Resource sharing: Share files, printers, and applications.
- Communication: Exchange information between devices.
- Data sharing: Transfer data across systems.
- Reliability: Provide alternative communication paths.
- Scalability: Allow networks to grow.

Types of networks:

PAN (Personal Area Network):
A small network around an individual, such as Bluetooth-connected devices.

LAN (Local Area Network):
Connects devices within a limited geographical area, such as a home, office, or college.

MAN (Metropolitan Area Network):
Connects networks across a city or metropolitan area.

WAN (Wide Area Network):
Connects networks across large geographical areas. The Internet is the largest example.

2. NETWORK TOPOLOGIES

Network topology describes how devices and communication links are arranged.

Bus topology:
All devices share a common communication cable.
Advantages: Simple and inexpensive.
Disadvantages: Failure of the main cable can affect the entire network.

Star topology:
All devices connect to a central hub or switch.
Advantages: Easy to manage and troubleshoot.
Disadvantages: Failure of the central device affects communication.

Ring topology:
Each device connects to two neighbouring devices, forming a ring.
Advantages: Organized data transmission.
Disadvantages: A link or device failure can disrupt a basic ring.

Mesh topology:
Devices are connected through multiple paths.
Advantages: High reliability and fault tolerance.
Disadvantages: Expensive and complex to install.

Tree topology:
A hierarchical arrangement of interconnected star networks.
Advantages: Scalable and suitable for large networks.
Disadvantages: Failure of an upper-level device may affect an entire branch.

3. NETWORK ARCHITECTURE

Client-server architecture:
A client requests services or resources from a centralized server.

Peer-to-peer architecture:
Devices communicate and share resources directly without requiring a dedicated central server.

4. NETWORK PROTOCOLS

A network protocol is a set of rules that defines how devices communicate and exchange data.

Protocols define:
- Data format.
- Addressing.
- Error handling.
- Transmission rules.
- Connection management.

Examples:
- HTTP and HTTPS: Web communication.
- TCP: Reliable, connection-oriented transport.
- UDP: Connectionless transport with low overhead.
- IP: Logical addressing and packet forwarding.
- DNS: Converts domain names into IP addresses.
- FTP: File transfer.
- SMTP: Sending email.
- SSH: Secure remote access.

5. OSI REFERENCE MODEL

The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model is a seven-layer conceptual framework that explains network communication.

Layer 7 - Application:
Provides network services to user applications.
Examples: HTTP, FTP, SMTP, DNS.

Layer 6 - Presentation:
Handles data translation, encryption, decryption, and compression.

Layer 5 - Session:
Establishes, manages, and terminates communication sessions.

Layer 4 - Transport:
Provides end-to-end communication, segmentation, flow control, and error recovery.
Examples: TCP and UDP.

Layer 3 - Network:
Handles logical addressing and routing of packets.
Example: IP.

Layer 2 - Data Link:
Provides framing, MAC addressing, and link-level error detection.
Examples: Ethernet and Wi-Fi link protocols.

Layer 1 - Physical:
Transmits raw bits through physical communication media.
Examples: Cables, connectors, and radio signals.

Data encapsulation:
Application data -> Segment -> Packet -> Frame -> Bits.

At the receiving device, decapsulation reverses this process.

6. TCP/IP MODEL

The TCP/IP model describes the architecture used by the Internet.

Application layer:
Provides application-level network services.
Examples: HTTP, HTTPS, DNS, SMTP.

Transport layer:
Provides process-to-process communication.
Examples: TCP and UDP.

Internet layer:
Handles logical addressing and routing.
Example: IP.

Network Access or Link layer:
Handles transmission over the local network and physical medium.
Examples: Ethernet and Wi-Fi.

OSI vs TCP/IP:
- OSI has seven layers.
- TCP/IP is commonly represented using four layers.
- OSI is a conceptual reference model.
- TCP/IP describes the practical protocol architecture used by the Internet.

7. TRANSMISSION MEDIA

Transmission media carry signals between network devices.

Guided media:
Signals travel through a physical medium.

Twisted pair cable:
Contains pairs of insulated copper wires twisted together.
Commonly used in Ethernet LANs.

Coaxial cable:
Contains a central conductor surrounded by insulation and shielding.

Optical fibre:
Transmits data using light.
Advantages include high bandwidth, low signal loss over long distances, and resistance to electromagnetic interference.

Unguided media:
Signals travel through wireless communication channels.

Radio waves:
Used in Wi-Fi and mobile communication.

Microwaves:
Used in point-to-point and satellite communication.

Infrared:
Used for short-range communication.

8. NETWORK DEVICES

Hub:
Connects devices and broadcasts incoming data to all ports. It operates at the physical layer.

Switch:
Connects devices in a LAN and forwards Ethernet frames using MAC addresses. It primarily operates at the data link layer.

Router:
Connects different networks and forwards packets using IP addresses. It operates at the network layer.

Repeater:
Regenerates signals to extend transmission distance.

Bridge:
Connects LAN segments and filters frames using MAC addresses.

Gateway:
Connects systems or networks that may use different protocols or architectures.

Modem:
Modulates and demodulates signals to enable communication over a particular transmission medium.

Access Point:
Allows wireless devices to connect to a wired network through Wi-Fi.

9. MAC ADDRESS AND IP ADDRESS

MAC address:
A link-layer hardware address used for communication within a local network.
It is commonly represented as six hexadecimal pairs.

Example:
00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E

IP address:
A logical address used to identify an interface and support communication across networks.

Two major IP versions:
- IPv4.
- IPv6.

MAC addresses operate at the data link layer, whereas IP addresses operate at the network layer.

10. IPv4 ADDRESSING

IPv4 uses a 32-bit address represented as four decimal octets separated by dots.

Example:
192.168.1.10

Each octet contains 8 bits and has a decimal range from 0 to 255.

An IPv4 address contains:
- Network portion.
- Host portion.

The subnet mask identifies which bits represent the network portion.

Example:
IP address: 192.168.1.10
Subnet mask: 255.255.255.0
CIDR notation: /24

11. IPv4 ADDRESS CLASSES

Traditional classful IPv4 addressing:

Class A:
First octet range: 1 to 126.
Default mask: 255.0.0.0.
Designed for large networks.

Class B:
First octet range: 128 to 191.
Default mask: 255.255.0.0.
Designed for medium-sized networks.

Class C:
First octet range: 192 to 223.
Default mask: 255.255.255.0.
Designed for smaller networks.

Class D:
First octet range: 224 to 239.
Used for multicast.

Class E:
First octet range: 240 to 255.
Reserved for experimental or special purposes.

Modern networks primarily use CIDR rather than traditional classful allocation.

12. PRIVATE AND PUBLIC IP ADDRESSES

Private IPv4 addresses are used within private networks and are not directly routed across the public Internet.

Private IPv4 ranges:
- 10.0.0.0 to 10.255.255.255.
- 172.16.0.0 to 172.31.255.255.
- 192.168.0.0 to 192.168.255.255.

A public IP address is globally routable on the Internet, subject to network and routing policies.

13. SUBNETTING

Subnetting divides a larger IP network into smaller logical networks.

Benefits:
- Efficient address allocation.
- Reduced broadcast domains.
- Better network organization.
- Easier network management.

A subnet mask separates network bits from host bits.

Example:
Network: 192.168.1.0/24

A /24 prefix means that the first 24 bits identify the network.

The remaining 8 bits are available for host addressing.

Total addresses = 2^8 = 256.
Traditionally usable host addresses = 256 - 2 = 254.

The two excluded addresses are the network address and broadcast address in a conventional IPv4 subnet.

14. CIDR

Classless Inter-Domain Routing (CIDR) allows IP addresses to be allocated using variable-length network prefixes.

Example:
192.168.1.0/26

A /26 prefix leaves 6 host bits.

Total addresses = 2^6 = 64.
Traditionally usable host addresses = 64 - 2 = 62.

CIDR reduces address wastage and supports flexible network allocation.

15. IPv6 ADDRESSING

IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses.

Example:
2001:db8::1

Advantages:
- Very large address space.
- Simplified address configuration.
- Improved support for modern network architecture.
- No broadcast addressing; multicast and anycast are used for group and selected-recipient communication.

IPv6 addresses are written using hexadecimal groups separated by colons.

16. TCP - TRANSMISSION CONTROL PROTOCOL

TCP is a connection-oriented transport-layer protocol that provides reliable and ordered data delivery.

Features:
- Connection establishment.
- Reliable data transmission.
- Sequence numbers.
- Acknowledgements.
- Retransmission of lost data.
- Flow control.
- Congestion control.
- Error detection.

TCP three-way handshake:

Step 1: Client sends SYN.
Step 2: Server responds with SYN-ACK.
Step 3: Client sends ACK.

After these steps, the TCP connection is established.

TCP is commonly used for web communication, email, and file transfer when reliable delivery is required.

17. UDP - USER DATAGRAM PROTOCOL

UDP is a connectionless transport-layer protocol.

Features:
- No connection establishment.
- Low protocol overhead.
- No built-in guarantee of delivery.
- No built-in guarantee of ordering.
- No retransmission mechanism in the protocol itself.

Applications include:
- DNS queries.
- Online gaming.
- Live audio and video.
- Real-time communication.

18. TCP VS UDP

TCP:
- Connection-oriented.
- Reliable and ordered delivery.
- Uses acknowledgements and retransmissions.
- Has flow and congestion control.
- Higher protocol overhead.

UDP:
- Connectionless.
- No built-in reliability or ordering guarantee.
- No built-in retransmission.
- Lower protocol overhead.
- Suitable for latency-sensitive applications.

19. PORT NUMBERS

A port number identifies a specific application or service endpoint on a host.

Common port numbers:
- HTTP: 80.
- HTTPS: 443.
- FTP control: 21.
- SSH: 22.
- SMTP: 25.
- DNS: 53.
- DHCP server: 67.
- DHCP client: 68.
- POP3: 110.
- IMAP: 143.

TCP and UDP use port numbers to identify application endpoints.

20. DNS - DOMAIN NAME SYSTEM

DNS translates human-readable domain names into IP addresses.

Example:
A user enters www.example.com.
The DNS resolver obtains the corresponding IP address.
The browser can then communicate with the destination server.

DNS hierarchy:
- Root DNS servers.
- Top-Level Domain servers.
- Authoritative DNS servers.

Common DNS record types:
- A: Maps a name to an IPv4 address.
- AAAA: Maps a name to an IPv6 address.
- CNAME: Provides an alias for another domain name.
- MX: Identifies mail servers.
- NS: Identifies authoritative name servers.
- TXT: Stores text information associated with a domain.

21. DHCP - DYNAMIC HOST CONFIGURATION PROTOCOL

DHCP automatically provides network configuration to devices.

It can assign:
- IP address.
- Subnet mask.
- Default gateway.
- DNS server information.

DHCP DORA process:
1. Discover: Client broadcasts a DHCP Discover message.
2. Offer: Server offers an IP configuration.
3. Request: Client requests the offered configuration.
4. Acknowledge: Server confirms the assignment.

22. HTTP AND HTTPS

HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol):
An application-layer protocol used for communication between web clients and servers.

HTTPS:
HTTP protected using TLS encryption.

HTTP methods:
- GET: Retrieve a resource.
- POST: Submit data or request resource creation.
- PUT: Create or replace a resource at a specified URI.

Common HTTP status codes:

23. ROUTING

Routing is the process of selecting paths through which packets travel from a source network to a destination network.

A router uses a routing table to determine the next hop or outgoing interface.

Types of routing:
- Static routing: Routes are configured manually.

Examples of routing protocols:
- RIP.

24. NAT - NETWORK ADDRESS TRANSLATION

NAT translates IP addresses between network address spaces.

A common use is allowing multiple devices with private IP addresses to access the Internet through a public IP address.

PAT (Port Address Translation) uses port numbers to distinguish connections sharing an external IP address.

Benefits:

25. NETWORK SECURITY

Network security protects networks, devices, and data against unauthorized access, misuse, modification, and attacks.

Common security principles:

- Integrity: Information remains accurate and unaltered without authorization.
- Availability: Systems and data remain accessible when required.

Common threats:

- Phishing.
- Denial-of-Service attacks.
- Man-in-the-Middle attacks.
- Packet sniffing.
- Spoofing.

Security mechanisms:
- Firewalls.

- Authentication.

A firewall monitors and controls incoming and outgoing network traffic according to defined security rules.

Types:

﻿DATA STRUCTURES

A data structure is a way of organizing and storing data so that it can be accessed, modified, and processed efficiently.

Data structures are classified into linear and non-linear structures. Linear structures organize elements sequentially, while non-linear structures organize elements hierarchically or as networks.

An Abstract Data Type (ADT) defines the behaviour and operations of a data structure without specifying its internal implementation.

An array is a collection of elements stored in indexed positions. Array elements are commonly stored in contiguous memory locations.

Array indexing allows an element to be accessed using its position. In a zero-indexed array, the first element is located at index zero.

Array traversal means visiting each element of an array to perform an operation.

Array insertion adds a new element at a specified position. Inserting into the middle of an array may require shifting existing elements.

Array deletion removes an element from a specified position. Elements following the deleted position may need to be shifted.

A multidimensional array is an array containing other arrays. A two-dimensional array is commonly used to represent a matrix.

A linked list is a linear data structure consisting of nodes connected through references or pointers.

A node in a singly linked list contains data and a reference to the next node.

The head of a linked list refers to its first node. The last node usually contains a null reference as its next pointer.

A singly linked list allows traversal in one direction.

A doubly linked list contains references to both the previous and next nodes. It supports traversal in both directions.

A circular linked list connects the last node back to the first node.

Linked list insertion can be performed at the beginning, end, or a specified position. The exact time complexity depends on whether the insertion position is already known.

Linked list deletion removes a node by adjusting the references connecting neighbouring nodes.

A stack is a linear data structure that follows the Last In First Out principle, also called LIFO.

Push is the operation that inserts an element onto the top of a stack.

Pop removes and returns the top element of a stack.

Peek or top returns the top element without removing it.

Stack overflow occurs when an insertion is attempted into a full fixed-capacity stack.

Stack underflow occurs when a removal is attempted from an empty stack.

Stacks are used in function calls, recursion, expression evaluation, undo operations, and backtracking.

A queue is a linear data structure that commonly follows the First In First Out principle, also called FIFO.

Enqueue inserts an element at the rear of a queue.

Dequeue removes an element from the front of a queue.

A circular queue connects the logical end of its storage back to the beginning. It allows efficient reuse of available array positions.

A priority queue removes elements according to their priority rather than simply their insertion order.

A deque is a double-ended queue that supports insertion and deletion at both ends.

A tree is a non-linear hierarchical data structure consisting of nodes connected by edges.

The root is the topmost node of a tree. A parent node has one or more child nodes.

A leaf node is a node that has no children.

The height of a tree is the number of edges on the longest downward path from the root to a leaf.

The depth of a node is the number of edges from the root to that node.

A binary tree is a tree in which each node has at most two children, commonly called the left child and right child.

A full binary tree is a binary tree in which every node has either zero or two children.

A complete binary tree has every level completely filled except possibly the last, which is filled from left to right.

A binary search tree is a binary tree in which values in the left subtree are smaller than the node value and values in the right subtree are larger, assuming distinct keys.

Searching in a balanced binary search tree takes logarithmic time on average and in the worst case. An unbalanced tree can require linear time.

Tree traversal is the process of visiting every node in a tree.

Preorder traversal visits the root, then the left subtree, and finally the right subtree.

Inorder traversal visits the left subtree, then the root, and finally the right subtree.

Postorder traversal visits the left subtree, then the right subtree, and finally the root.

Level-order traversal visits tree nodes level by level, commonly using a queue.

A heap is a complete binary tree that satisfies the heap property.

A max heap maintains the property that every parent is greater than or equal to its children.

A min heap maintains the property that every parent is less than or equal to its children.

A heap is commonly used to implement priority queues.

A hash table stores key-value pairs using a hash function that maps keys to array positions or buckets.

A hash function converts a key into a hash value used to determine where the associated data should be stored.

A collision occurs when two different keys map to the same hash position.

Collision resolution techniques include separate chaining and open addressing.

Separate chaining stores multiple entries that map to the same position in a collection associated with that position.

Open addressing searches for another available position within the hash table when a collision occurs.

A graph is a non-linear data structure consisting of vertices and edges.

A vertex represents an entity in a graph, while an edge represents a connection between two vertices.

An undirected graph has edges without a specified direction.

A directed graph has edges with a specified direction.

A weighted graph assigns a weight or cost to its edges.

An adjacency matrix represents a graph using a two-dimensional matrix. Its entries indicate whether an edge exists between pairs of vertices or store edge weights.

An adjacency list represents each vertex together with a list of its neighbouring vertices.

Breadth First Search (BFS) explores a graph level by level using a queue.

Depth First Search (DFS) explores a graph by following a path as deeply as possible before backtracking. It can be implemented using recursion or an explicit stack.

A connected graph is an undirected graph in which a path exists between every pair of vertices.

A cycle is a path that starts and ends at the same vertex without repeating other vertices.

TIME AND SPACE COMPLEXITY

Time complexity describes how the running time of an algorithm grows as the input size increases.

Space complexity describes how memory requirements grow as the input size increases.

Big O notation describes an asymptotic upper bound on the growth of an algorithm's resource requirements.

O(1) represents constant time complexity. The number of operations does not grow with input size.

O(log n) represents logarithmic time complexity. The problem size is repeatedly reduced by a constant factor.

O(n) represents linear time complexity. The number of operations grows proportionally to input size.

O(n log n) commonly occurs in efficient comparison-based sorting algorithms such as merge sort and average-case heap sort.

O(n squared) represents quadratic time complexity and commonly occurs in algorithms with two nested loops over the input.

QUESTION: What is a data structure?
ANSWER: A data structure is a method of organizing and storing data so that operations such as searching, insertion, deletion, and traversal can be performed efficiently.

QUESTION: What is the difference between an array and a linked list?
ANSWER: An array stores elements in indexed positions and commonly uses contiguous memory. A linked list stores elements in separate nodes connected through references. Arrays provide constant-time indexed access, while linked lists require traversal to access an element by position.

QUESTION: What is the difference between a stack and a queue?
ANSWER: A stack follows Last In First Out, meaning the most recently inserted element is removed first. A queue follows First In First Out, meaning the earliest inserted element is removed first.

QUESTION: What are the basic operations of a stack?
ANSWER: Push inserts an element, pop removes the top element, and peek returns the top element without removing it.

QUESTION: What are the applications of a stack?
ANSWER: Stacks are used in recursion, function call management, expression evaluation, undo operations, browser history, and backtracking algorithms.

QUESTION: What is a circular queue?
ANSWER: A circular queue is a queue in which the last position is logically connected to the first position. It allows previously occupied positions to be reused after elements are removed.

QUESTION: What is a binary tree?
ANSWER: A binary tree is a hierarchical data structure in which each node can have at most two children, known as the left child and right child.

QUESTION: What is a binary search tree?
ANSWER: A binary search tree is a binary tree in which values in the left subtree are smaller than the node value and values in the right subtree are larger, assuming distinct keys.

QUESTION: What is the difference between BFS and DFS?
ANSWER: BFS explores neighbouring vertices level by level using a queue. DFS explores a path deeply before backtracking and commonly uses recursion or a stack.

QUESTION: What is a hash collision?
ANSWER: A hash collision occurs when two different keys produce the same hash-table position. Collision resolution techniques include separate chaining and open addressing.

QUESTION: What is the difference between a min heap and a max heap?
ANSWER: A min heap maintains the smallest element at the root, while a max heap maintains the largest element at the root.

QUESTION: What is the difference between an adjacency matrix and an adjacency list?
ANSWER: An adjacency matrix represents graph connections using a two-dimensional matrix. An adjacency list stores the neighbours of each vertex. Adjacency matrices use O(V squared) space, while adjacency lists generally use O(V + E) space.

QUESTION: What is Big O notation?
ANSWER: Big O notation describes an asymptotic upper bound on the growth of an algorithm's resource requirements as input size increases.

QUESTION: What is the time complexity of accessing an array element by index?
ANSWER: Accessing an array element by index takes O(1) time because its location can be calculated directly.

QUESTION: What is the time complexity of searching for an element in an unsorted array?
ANSWER: Linear search takes O(n) time in the worst case because it may need to examine every element.

﻿DATABASE MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS (DBMS)

A database is an organized collection of related data that can be stored, accessed, modified, and managed efficiently.

A Database Management System (DBMS) is software that allows users and applications to create, store, retrieve, update, and manage databases.

A database system consists of the database, DBMS software, users, applications, and supporting hardware.

A DBMS provides data storage, retrieval, security, integrity, concurrency control, and backup facilities.

Data redundancy occurs when the same data is unnecessarily stored in multiple locations. It can increase storage requirements and create inconsistencies.

Data inconsistency occurs when different copies of the same information contain conflicting values.

Data integrity refers to the accuracy, validity, and consistency of stored data.

Data independence is the ability to change the database schema at one level without requiring changes at the next higher level.

The three-schema architecture separates a database into external, conceptual, and internal levels.

The external level describes how individual users or applications view the data.

The conceptual level describes the overall logical structure of the database, including entities, relationships, and constraints.

The internal level describes how data is physically stored and organized.

A database schema defines the logical structure of a database, including tables, attributes, relationships, and constraints.

A database instance is the actual data stored in the database at a particular moment.

The relational model represents data using relations, commonly implemented as tables.

A relation is a table consisting of rows and columns.

A tuple is a row in a relational table.

An attribute is a named column in a relation.

A domain defines the permitted set of values for an attribute.

A candidate key is a minimal set of attributes that uniquely identifies every tuple in a relation.

A primary key is the candidate key selected to uniquely identify rows in a table. It must be unique and cannot contain NULL values.

A foreign key is an attribute or set of attributes that references a candidate key, usually the primary key, in another or the same table.

A super key is a set of one or more attributes that uniquely identifies a tuple. It may contain unnecessary attributes.

A composite key consists of two or more attributes that together uniquely identify a tuple.

A unique constraint prevents duplicate values in the constrained column or combination of columns. NULL handling depends on the database system.

Entity integrity requires that primary key values are unique and not NULL.

Referential integrity ensures that foreign key values refer to valid referenced rows, subject to the defined constraints.

An Entity Relationship (ER) model represents real-world entities, their attributes, and relationships.

An entity is a distinguishable real-world object, such as a student, employee, or department.

An entity set is a collection of similar entities.

An attribute describes a property of an entity. Examples include student name, roll number, and age.

A simple attribute cannot be divided into smaller meaningful components.

A composite attribute can be divided into smaller components. For example, an address may contain street, city, and postal code.

A multivalued attribute can contain multiple values for a single entity.

A derived attribute is calculated from other stored attributes. For example, age can be derived from date of birth.

A relationship represents an association between entities.

A one-to-one relationship associates one entity in one set with at most one entity in another set, and vice versa.

A one-to-many relationship allows one entity to be associated with multiple entities in another set.

A many-to-many relationship allows multiple entities on both sides to be associated with one another.

An ER diagram graphically represents entities, attributes, and relationships.

SQL

Structured Query Language (SQL) is used to define, manipulate, retrieve, and control data in relational database systems.

Data Definition Language (DDL) defines or modifies database structures. CREATE, ALTER, and DROP are common DDL commands.

Data Manipulation Language (DML) inserts, modifies, and deletes records. INSERT, UPDATE, and DELETE are common DML commands.

Data Query Language (DQL) retrieves information from tables. SELECT is its primary command.

Data Control Language (DCL) manages database privileges. GRANT and REVOKE are common DCL commands.

Transaction Control Language (TCL) manages transactions. COMMIT, ROLLBACK, and SAVEPOINT are common TCL commands.

The CREATE TABLE statement creates a new table with specified columns and constraints.

The INSERT statement adds new records to a table.

The SELECT statement retrieves data from one or more tables.

The WHERE clause filters records based on a condition.

The ORDER BY clause sorts query results in ascending or descending order.

The GROUP BY clause groups rows that share common values in specified columns.

The HAVING clause filters groups after grouping and aggregation.

The UPDATE statement modifies existing records in a table.

The DELETE statement removes selected records from a table.

The DROP TABLE statement removes a table definition and its associated data.

The ALTER TABLE statement changes the structure of an existing table.

The DISTINCT keyword removes duplicate rows from a query result.

Aggregate functions perform calculations over groups of values. COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, and MAX are common aggregate functions.

A SQL join combines related rows from two or more tables based on a specified relationship or condition.

An INNER JOIN returns rows that satisfy the join condition in both tables.

A LEFT JOIN returns all rows from the left table and matching rows from the right table. Unmatched right-side columns contain NULL values.

A RIGHT JOIN returns all rows from the right table and matching rows from the left table. Unmatched left-side columns contain NULL values.

A FULL OUTER JOIN returns matching rows and unmatched rows from both tables, with NULL values for missing matches.

A CROSS JOIN returns the Cartesian product of two tables.

A self join joins a table with itself, commonly using table aliases.

A subquery is a query nested inside another SQL statement.

A correlated subquery refers to columns from an outer query and is evaluated in relation to the outer query.

A view is a virtual table defined by a query. It can simplify complex queries and restrict access to selected data.

An index is a database structure that helps retrieve rows more efficiently. Indexes require additional storage and can increase the cost of data modifications.

DATABASE NORMALIZATION

Normalization is the process of organizing relational tables to reduce unnecessary data redundancy and prevent undesirable modification anomalies.

An insertion anomaly occurs when a record cannot be inserted without adding unrelated information.

An update anomaly occurs when the same fact must be updated in multiple locations, potentially producing inconsistent data.

A deletion anomaly occurs when deleting one record unintentionally removes other useful information.

A functional dependency describes a relationship in which one set of attributes determines another set of attributes.

In a functional dependency X determines Y, written X -> Y, whenever two tuples agreeing on X must also agree on Y.

First Normal Form (1NF) requires each attribute value to be atomic within the relational model and prohibits repeating groups.

Second Normal Form (2NF) requires a relation to be in 1NF and every non-prime attribute to be fully functionally dependent on every candidate key, with no partial dependency.

Third Normal Form (3NF) requires a relation to be in 2NF and to have no inappropriate transitive dependency of non-prime attributes on candidate keys. Formally, for every non-trivial functional dependency X -> A, either X is a super key or A is a prime attribute.

Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) requires every non-trivial functional dependency X -> Y to have X as a super key.

Decomposition divides a relation into smaller relations to reduce redundancy.

Lossless decomposition ensures that joining decomposed relations reconstructs exactly the original relation without introducing spurious tuples.

Dependency preservation means that functional dependencies can be enforced on the decomposed relations without requiring joins.

DATABASE TRANSACTIONS

A transaction is a logical unit of database operations that must be managed as a single unit of work.

The ACID properties describe important guarantees of reliable database transactions.

Atomicity means a transaction completes entirely or has no effect on the database.

Consistency means a transaction preserves defined database rules and constraints.

Isolation means concurrent transactions are controlled so that their execution does not produce unacceptable interference.

Durability means committed changes persist even after failures, subject to the database's durability guarantees.

COMMIT permanently records the successful changes of a transaction.

ROLLBACK cancels uncommitted changes made by a transaction.

SAVEPOINT establishes a point within a transaction to which a partial rollback can be performed.

Concurrency control manages simultaneous transactions to preserve database correctness.

A dirty read occurs when a transaction reads data written by another transaction that has not yet committed.

A non-repeatable read occurs when the same row is read twice by one transaction and another committed transaction changes that row between the reads.

A phantom read occurs when repeating a query returns a different set of rows because another transaction inserted or deleted matching rows.

Serializability is the correctness criterion that requires concurrent transaction execution to produce an effect equivalent to some serial execution.

A schedule is a sequence of operations from one or more transactions.

A deadlock occurs when transactions wait indefinitely for resources held by one another.

A database lock controls access to a data item during concurrent transaction execution.

A shared lock generally permits multiple transactions to read the same data item.

An exclusive lock generally prevents other transactions from acquiring conflicting locks on the same data item.

﻿JAVA PROGRAMMING

Java is a high-level, class-based, object-oriented programming language. It is designed to be portable, secure, and suitable for developing different types of applications.

Java source code is written in files with the .java extension. The Java compiler converts source code into bytecode, which is executed by the Java Virtual Machine.

The Java Development Kit (JDK) provides tools required to develop Java applications. It includes the Java compiler, runtime components, and development utilities.

The Java Runtime Environment (JRE) provides the runtime components required to execute Java applications. The JVM is responsible for executing Java bytecode.

The Java Virtual Machine is an abstract computing machine that executes Java bytecode. It provides platform independence by allowing compatible JVM implementations to execute the same bytecode on different operating systems.

The Java compilation process converts a Java source file into bytecode. The java launcher starts the JVM to execute the compiled program.

Java is platform independent because compiled bytecode can run on any compatible system with a JVM.

A class is a blueprint that defines the properties and behaviours of objects. It contains fields, constructors, and methods.

An object is an instance of a class. It has state represented by fields and behaviour represented by methods.

A field is a variable declared inside a class or object. It stores the state of an object or class.

A method is a block of code that performs a particular operation. Methods support code reuse and modularity.

A constructor is a special member used to initialize objects. Its name matches the class name, and it has no return type.

A default constructor is a constructor provided automatically by the compiler when no constructor is explicitly declared.

A parameterized constructor accepts arguments to initialize an object with specific values.

The this keyword refers to the current object. It can be used to access instance members and distinguish instance variables from parameters.

The static keyword declares members that belong to the class rather than individual objects. Static variables are shared among instances of the class.

The final keyword can prevent a variable from being reassigned, a method from being overridden, or a class from being extended.

OBJECT ORIENTED PROGRAMMING

Object-oriented programming is a programming approach based on classes and objects. Its major principles are encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, and abstraction.

Encapsulation combines data and methods within a class and controls access to internal data using access modifiers. It helps protect an object's state.

Inheritance allows one class to acquire accessible properties and methods from another class. Java uses the extends keyword for class inheritance.

The parent class is the class whose accessible members are inherited. The child class is the class that extends another class.

Java supports single, multilevel, and hierarchical class inheritance. A Java class cannot directly extend multiple classes.

Polymorphism means the ability to represent different behaviours through a common interface or reference. Method overloading and method overriding are important forms of polymorphism.

Method overloading occurs when multiple methods in the same class have the same name but different parameter lists. The compiler determines which overloaded method to call based on the arguments.

Method overriding occurs when a subclass provides its own implementation of an inherited method with a compatible method signature.

Abstraction means exposing essential functionality while hiding implementation details. Java supports abstraction through abstract classes and interfaces.

An abstract class is declared using the abstract keyword. It cannot be instantiated directly and may contain abstract as well as concrete methods.

An interface defines a contract that implementing classes must follow. A class uses the implements keyword to implement an interface.

An abstract method is a method declared without an implementation. Concrete subclasses must implement inherited abstract methods unless they are also abstract.

An access modifier controls the visibility of classes, methods, constructors, and fields. Java provides public, protected, private, and package-private access levels.

Exception handling allows a program to detect and manage exceptional conditions so that it can respond appropriately.

Java uses try, catch, finally, throw, and throws keywords for exception handling.

﻿OPERATING SYSTEMS

1. INTRODUCTION TO OPERATING SYSTEMS

An Operating System (OS) is system software that acts as an interface between users and computer hardware. It manages hardware resources and provides services to application programs.

Main objectives:
- Convenience: Makes computers easier to use.
- Efficiency: Utilizes hardware resources effectively.
- Resource management: Allocates CPU, memory, storage, and I/O devices.
- Security: Protects data and system resources.

Examples include Windows, Linux, macOS, Android, and Unix.

2. FUNCTIONS OF AN OPERATING SYSTEM

The major functions of an OS are:
- Process management: Creates, schedules, and terminates processes.
- Memory management: Allocates and deallocates main memory.
- File management: Organizes files and directories.
- Device management: Controls input and output devices.
- Security management: Controls access to resources.
- User interface: Provides command-line or graphical interaction.
- Resource allocation: Distributes resources among programs.
- Error detection: Detects and handles hardware and software errors.

3. TYPES OF OPERATING SYSTEMS

Batch Operating System:
Jobs are collected and executed in batches without direct interaction during execution.

Multiprogramming Operating System:
Multiple programs are kept in memory. The CPU switches to another job when one job waits for I/O.

Multitasking Operating System:
Allows multiple tasks to make progress during the same period by sharing CPU time.

Time-Sharing Operating System:
CPU time is divided into small time slices and shared among multiple users or processes.

Multiprocessing Operating System:
Uses two or more processors or CPU cores to execute processes.

Distributed Operating System:
Manages multiple networked computers and makes them appear as a coordinated system.

Real-Time Operating System:
Provides responses within specified timing constraints.
Hard real-time systems must meet critical deadlines. Soft real-time systems aim to meet deadlines but may tolerate occasional delays.

4. KERNEL AND SYSTEM CALLS

The kernel is the central component of an operating system. It manages CPU scheduling, memory, devices, and system resources.

Kernel responsibilities:
- Process management.
- Memory management.
- Device management.
- Interrupt handling.
- System call handling.

A system call is a mechanism through which a user program requests a service from the operating system kernel.

Categories of system calls:
- Process control: Create, execute, and terminate processes.
- File management: Create, read, write, and delete files.
- Device management: Request and release devices.
- Information maintenance: Obtain system information.
- Communication: Exchange information between processes.
- Protection: Control access permissions.

5. PROCESS CONCEPT

A process is a program in execution. It includes program code, data, stack, heap, CPU registers, and other execution information.

A program is a passive set of instructions stored on disk, whereas a process is an active execution of those instructions.

Process states:
- New: The process is being created.
- Ready: The process is waiting for CPU allocation.
- Running: The process is currently executing.
- Waiting or Blocked: The process is waiting for an event or I/O operation.
- Terminated: The process has completed execution.

Typical process state transitions:
New -> Ready -> Running -> Terminated.
Running -> Waiting -> Ready.
Running -> Ready when preempted by the scheduler.

6. PROCESS CONTROL BLOCK (PCB)

A Process Control Block is a data structure maintained by the operating system to store information about a process.

A PCB commonly contains:
- Process ID: Unique process identifier.
- Process state: Current state of the process.
- Program counter: Address of the next instruction.
- CPU registers: Register values of the process.
- CPU scheduling information: Priority and scheduling data.
- Memory management information: Memory allocation details.
- Accounting information: CPU usage and other statistics.
- I/O status information: Open files and allocated devices.

The operating system uses the PCB during process scheduling and context switching.

7. CONTEXT SWITCHING

Context switching is the process of saving the CPU state of one process and restoring the state of another process.

Steps:
1. An interrupt or scheduling event occurs.
2. The operating system saves the current process state in its PCB.
3. The scheduler selects another ready process.
4. The operating system restores the selected process state.
5. The CPU resumes execution of the selected process.

Context switching enables multitasking but introduces overhead because the CPU spends time switching rather than executing application instructions.

8. THREADS

A thread is the smallest unit of CPU execution within a process.

A process may contain multiple threads. Threads belonging to the same process share code, data, and resources, but each thread has its own stack, registers, and program counter.

Benefits:
- Responsiveness: One thread can continue while another waits.
- Resource sharing: Threads share process resources.
- Economy: Creating threads is generally less expensive than creating processes.
- Parallelism: Threads can execute simultaneously on multiple CPU cores.

Types of threads:
- User-level threads: Managed by a user-level thread library.
- Kernel-level threads: Managed by the operating system kernel.

9. CPU SCHEDULING

CPU scheduling is the process of selecting a process from the ready queue and allocating the CPU to it.

Objectives:
- Maximize CPU utilization.
- Maximize throughput.
- Minimize turnaround time.
- Minimize waiting time.
- Minimize response time.

Scheduling algorithms:

First Come First Served (FCFS):
Processes are executed in the order in which they arrive. It is generally non-preemptive and may cause the convoy effect.

Shortest Job First (SJF):
The process with the shortest estimated CPU burst is selected first. It can be non-preemptive.

Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF):
Preemptive version of SJF. The process with the smallest remaining CPU burst is selected.

Priority Scheduling:
The CPU is allocated to the process with the highest priority. It may be preemptive or non-preemptive. Starvation can occur.

Round Robin (RR):
Each process receives a fixed time quantum. If it does not finish within the quantum, it returns to the ready queue.

10. CPU SCHEDULING TERMS

Arrival Time:
The time at which a process enters the ready queue.

Burst Time:
The amount of CPU time required by a process.

Completion Time:
The time at which a process finishes execution.

Turnaround Time:
Turnaround Time = Completion Time - Arrival Time.

Waiting Time:
Waiting Time = Turnaround Time - Burst Time.

Response Time:
Response Time = First CPU Start Time - Arrival Time.

Throughput:
Number of processes completed per unit of time.

CPU Utilization:
Percentage of time the CPU is busy executing processes.

11. PROCESS SYNCHRONIZATION

Process synchronization coordinates concurrent processes or threads that share data and resources.

A race condition occurs when the result of program execution depends on the unpredictable timing or order of concurrent operations.

A critical section is the part of a program where shared data or resources are accessed.

A correct critical-section solution should satisfy:
- Mutual exclusion: Only one process can execute the critical section at a time.
- Progress: If no process is in the critical section, the selection of the next process should not be postponed indefinitely.
- Bounded waiting: A process should not wait indefinitely to enter its critical section.

12. SEMAPHORES

A semaphore is a synchronization variable used to control access to shared resources.

Two fundamental operations:
- wait(): Decreases the semaphore value or blocks the process when the resource is unavailable.
- signal(): Increases the semaphore value or wakes a waiting process.

Types:
- Binary semaphore: Has two logical states, commonly used for mutual exclusion.
- Counting semaphore: Represents multiple available instances of a resource.

A mutex is a mutual-exclusion lock that allows only one thread to hold the lock at a time. Unlike a general semaphore, a mutex normally has ownership: the thread that locks it is responsible for unlocking it.

13. DEADLOCK

A deadlock is a situation in which a group of processes is permanently blocked because each process is waiting for a resource held by another process in the group.

Four necessary conditions for deadlock:
1. Mutual exclusion: At least one resource cannot be shared.
2. Hold and wait: A process holds resources while requesting additional resources.
3. No preemption: Resources cannot be forcibly taken away.
4. Circular wait: A circular chain of processes exists, with each waiting for a resource held by the next.

Deadlock handling methods:
- Prevention: Ensure at least one necessary condition cannot hold.
- Avoidance: Allocate resources only when the system remains in a safe state.
- Detection and recovery: Detect deadlocks and recover by terminating processes or preempting resources where possible.
- Ignore: Some systems assume deadlocks are sufficiently rare and do not handle them explicitly.

14. BANKER'S ALGORITHM

Banker's Algorithm is a deadlock-avoidance algorithm that checks whether granting a resource request leaves the system in a safe state.

A safe state is one in which there exists at least one sequence of process execution that allows every process to complete using the available resources and resources released by completed processes.

Important data structures:
- Available: Number of available instances of each resource.
- Max: Maximum resource requirement of each process.
- Allocation: Resources currently allocated to each process.
- Need: Remaining resource requirement.

Need = Max - Allocation.

The algorithm grants a request only if the resulting state is safe.

15. MEMORY MANAGEMENT

Memory management is the operating system function responsible for allocating and deallocating main memory to processes.

Responsibilities:
- Keep track of used and free memory.
- Allocate memory to processes.
- Deallocate memory after process completion.
- Protect process address spaces.
- Support virtual memory.

Contiguous memory allocation:
Each process is allocated one continuous block of physical memory.

Common placement strategies:
- First Fit: Selects the first sufficiently large free block.
- Best Fit: Selects the smallest sufficiently large free block.
- Worst Fit: Selects the largest available free block.

16. FRAGMENTATION

Internal fragmentation:
Wasted space inside an allocated memory block because the allocated block is larger than the requested memory.

External fragmentation:
Free memory is available, but it is divided into small non-contiguous blocks, making it difficult to satisfy a large allocation request.

Compaction can reduce external fragmentation by moving allocated blocks together, when the system supports it.

17. PAGING

Paging is a memory-management technique that divides logical memory into fixed-size pages and physical memory into fixed-size frames.

A page table maps virtual page numbers to physical frame numbers.

Logical address consists of:
- Page number.
- Page offset.

The page number is used to locate the corresponding frame through the page table. The offset identifies the location within that frame.

Advantages:
- Eliminates external fragmentation in physical memory allocation.
- Allows non-contiguous allocation of a process.
- Supports virtual memory.

Disadvantage:
- May cause internal fragmentation, especially in the last page.
- Page tables require memory.

18. SEGMENTATION

Segmentation divides a program into variable-sized logical units called segments.

Examples:
- Code segment.
- Data segment.
- Stack segment.
- Heap segment.

A logical address consists of a segment number and an offset.

The segment table stores the base address and limit of each segment.

Advantages:
- Reflects the logical structure of a program.
- Supports protection and sharing at segment level.

Disadvantage:
- Can cause external fragmentation because segments have variable sizes.

19. VIRTUAL MEMORY

Virtual memory is a memory-management technique that allows a process to execute without requiring all of its pages to be present in physical memory at the same time.

It provides each process with a virtual address space and uses secondary storage to support memory demands beyond available physical memory.

Advantages:
- Allows execution of programs larger than physical memory.
- Improves memory utilization.
- Supports more processes in memory.
- Provides process isolation.

20. DEMAND PAGING AND PAGE FAULT

Demand paging loads a page into physical memory only when it is needed.

A page fault occurs when a process accesses a page that is not currently present in physical memory.

Page fault handling:
1. The CPU references a page.
2. The memory-management hardware detects that the page is absent.
3. A page-fault trap transfers control to the operating system.
4. The OS checks whether the memory reference is valid.
5. The OS locates the required page on secondary storage.
6. A free frame is selected, or a victim page is replaced.
7. The required page is loaded into memory.
8. The page table is updated.
9. The interrupted instruction is restarted.

21. PAGE REPLACEMENT ALGORITHMS

When no free frame is available, the OS selects a page to remove.

FIFO (First In First Out):
Replaces the page that entered memory earliest. It may suffer from Belady's anomaly.

Optimal Page Replacement:
Replaces the page that will not be used for the longest time in the future. It provides the theoretical minimum number of page faults but requires future knowledge, so it is mainly used for comparison.

LRU (Least Recently Used):
Replaces the page that has not been used for the longest time in the past.

22. THRASHING

Thrashing is a condition in which the system spends most of its time handling page faults and swapping pages rather than executing useful instructions.

Causes:
- Insufficient physical memory.
- Too many active processes.
- High page-fault rate.
- Poor allocation of frames.

Methods to reduce thrashing:
- Reduce the degree of multiprogramming.
- Allocate sufficient frames.
- Use working-set or page-fault-frequency techniques.

23. FILE SYSTEM

A file system is the method used by an operating system to organize, store, retrieve, and manage files on storage devices.

File attributes:
- Name.
- Type.
- Size.
- Location.
- Protection permissions.
- Creation and modification timestamps.

File operations:
- Create.
- Open.
- Read.
- Write.
- Close.
- Delete.
- Rename.

Directory structures:
- Single-level directory.
- Two-level directory.
- Tree-structured directory.
- Acyclic graph directory.
- General graph directory.

24. DISK SCHEDULING

Disk scheduling determines the order in which pending disk I/O requests are serviced.

Algorithms:
- FCFS: Services requests in arrival order.
- SSTF: Services the request closest to the current disk-head position.
- SCAN: Disk head moves in one direction, servicing requests, then reverses direction.
- C-SCAN: Services requests in one direction and returns to the beginning without servicing requests during the return.
- LOOK: Similar to SCAN, but reverses at the last pending request rather than going to the physical disk end.
- C-LOOK: Similar to C-SCAN, but jumps between the last and first pending requests.

25. IMPORTANT DIFFERENCES

Process vs Thread:
A process is an independent program in execution with its own address space. A thread is an execution unit within a process and shares process resources with other threads.

Paging vs Segmentation:
Paging divides memory into fixed-size pages and frames. Segmentation divides programs into variable-sized logical segments.

Internal vs External Fragmentation:
Internal fragmentation is wasted space inside an allocated block. External fragmentation is free space scattered between allocated blocks.

Deadlock Prevention vs Avoidance:
Prevention ensures at least one necessary deadlock condition cannot occur. Avoidance checks resource allocation requests and grants them only when the system remains safe.

Preemptive vs Non-Preemptive Scheduling:
Preemptive scheduling can take the CPU away from a running process. Non-preemptive scheduling allows a process to continue until it terminates or blocks.

2. NUMBER SYSTEMS

Natural numbers:
Counting numbers such as 1, 2, 3, 4, and so on.

Whole numbers:
Natural numbers together with zero.

Integers:
Positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.

Rational numbers:
Numbers that can be expressed as p/q, where p and q are integers and q is not zero.

Examples: 1/2, -3/4, and 5.

Irrational numbers:
Numbers that cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers.

Examples: square root of 2 and pi.

Real numbers:
The collection of rational and irrational numbers.

Complex numbers:
Numbers expressed as a + bi, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit satisfying i^2 = -1.

Addition:
Combines two or more quantities.

Subtraction:
Finds the difference between quantities.

Order of operations:
- Parentheses.
- Exponents.

4. EXPONENTS AND POWERS

An exponent indicates how many times a base is multiplied by itself.

Example:
2^3 = 2 * 2 * 2 = 8.

Laws of exponents:

Product rule:
a^m * a^n = a^(m+n).

Quotient rule:
a^m / a^n = a^(m-n), for a not equal to zero.

Power rule:
(a^m)^n = a^(mn).

Power of a product:
(ab)^n = a^n * b^n.

Zero exponent:
a^0 = 1, for a not equal to zero.

Negative exponent:
a^(-n) = 1/a^n, for a not equal to zero.

Fractional exponent:
a^(1/n) represents the nth root of a when defined in the real numbers.

5. ALGEBRA

Algebra uses symbols and variables to represent unknown or changing quantities.

An algebraic expression contains variables, constants, and mathematical operations.

Example:
3x + 5.

A variable is a symbol representing a value that may be unknown or change.

A coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable.

A constant is a fixed value.

An equation states that two expressions are equal.

Example:

Solving:
2x = 10 - 4.
2x = 6.
x = 3.

6. LINEAR EQUATIONS

A linear equation in one variable can be written as:

ax + b = 0, where a is not zero.

Solution:
x = -b/a.

A linear equation in two variables can be written as:

ax + by = c.

Its graph is a straight line.

Example:
2x + y = 6.

When x = 0, y = 6.
When y = 0, x = 3.

7. QUADRATIC EQUATIONS

A quadratic equation has the general form:

ax^2 + bx + c = 0, where a is not zero.

The quadratic formula is:

x = (-b ± sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / 2a.

The discriminant is:

D = b^2 - 4ac.

Nature of roots:
- D > 0: Two distinct real roots.
- D = 0: Two equal real roots.
- D < 0: Two complex conjugate roots when coefficients are real.

Example:
x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0.

Factorization:
(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0.

Roots:
x = 2 and x = 3.

Polynomial operations:
- Addition.

9. FACTORIZATION

The inverse of a function reverses its input-output relationship.

12. LOGARITHMS

A logarithm is the inverse operation of exponentiation.

a^x = b,

then:

log_a(b) = x.

Conditions:
- a > 0.
- a is not equal to 1.

Logarithm rules:

log_a(x/y) = log_a(x) - log_a(y).

15. DETERMINANTS

A determinant is a scalar value associated with a square matrix.

A = [a b; c d]

- Interchanging two rows changes the sign of the determinant.
- A determinant is zero if two rows or columns are identical.
- The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal elements.
- det(AB) = det(A) * det(B).

A square matrix has an inverse if its determinant is non-zero.

16. MATRIX INVERSE

The inverse of a square matrix A is denoted by A^(-1).

It satisfies:

A * A^(-1) = I.

For a 2 x 2 matrix:

A = [a b; c d]

A^(-1) = 1/(ad - bc) * [d -b; -c a].

This formula is valid when ad - bc is not zero.

A limit describes the value approached by a function as its input approaches a particular value.

A function is continuous at x = a if:

23. DIFFERENTIATION

Differentiation determines the rate of change of a function with respect to a variable.

The derivative of f(x) is denoted by:

24. BASIC DIFFERENTIATION RULES

Constant rule:
d/dx(c) = 0.

Power rule:
d/dx(x^n) = n*x^(n-1).

Sum rule:
d/dx(f + g) = f' + g'.

Product rule:
d/dx(fg) = f'g + fg'.

Quotient rule:
d/dx(f/g) = (g f' - f g')/g^2, where g is not zero.

Chain rule:
d/dx[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) * g'(x).

25. COMMON DERIVATIVES

d/dx(sin x) = cos x.

d/dx(cos x) = -sin x.

d/dx(tan x) = sec^2 x.

d/dx(e^x) = e^x.

d/dx(ln x) = 1/x, for x > 0.

d/dx(a^x) = a^x ln(a), for a > 0.

26. APPLICATIONS OF DIFFERENTIATION

Differentiation is used to:
- Find velocity from displacement.
- Find acceleration from velocity.
- Determine increasing and decreasing intervals.
- Find stationary points.
- Solve optimization problems.
- Estimate small changes.
- Study rates of change in engineering systems.

A stationary point occurs where the first derivative is zero or undefined, subject to the function being defined there.

27. MAXIMA AND MINIMA

A maximum is a point where a function reaches a locally or globally highest value over the specified domain.

A minimum is a point where a function reaches a locally or globally lowest value over the specified domain.

First derivative test:
The sign change of the first derivative can help identify local maxima and minima.

Second derivative test:
At a stationary point:
- If f''(x) > 0, the function has a local minimum.
- If f''(x) < 0, the function has a local maximum.
- If f''(x) = 0, the test is inconclusive.

28. INTEGRATION

Integration is the process of finding an antiderivative or calculating accumulated quantities.

Indefinite integral:

Integral f(x) dx = F(x) + C.

Where:
F'(x) = f(x).

C is the constant of integration.

Definite integral:

Integral from a to b of f(x) dx.

It represents signed accumulation over the interval.

29. BASIC INTEGRATION RULES

Integral of a constant:

Integral c dx = cx + C.

Power rule:

Integral x^n dx = x^(n+1)/(n+1) + C, for n not equal to -1.

Integral of 1/x:

Integral (1/x) dx = ln|x| + C.

Integral of e^x:

Integral e^x dx = e^x + C.

Integral of sin x:

Integral sin x dx = -cos x + C.

Integral of cos x:

Integral cos x dx = sin x + C.

30. METHODS OF INTEGRATION

Substitution:
Replaces a suitable inner expression with a new variable to simplify an integral.

Integration by parts:

Integral u dv = uv - Integral v du.

Partial fractions:
Expresses a rational function as a sum of simpler fractions.

Trigonometric substitution:
Uses trigonometric identities to simplify certain integrals.

31. FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM OF CALCULUS

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects differentiation and integration.

If F'(x) = f(x), then:

Integral from a to b of f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a).

It allows definite integrals to be evaluated using antiderivatives.

32. APPLICATIONS OF INTEGRATION

Integration is used to calculate:
- Area under a curve.
- Displacement from velocity.
- Distance-related accumulated quantities under suitable conditions.
- Volume of solids.
- Work done by a variable force.
- Accumulated probability.
- Total change in engineering systems.

33. INTRODUCTION TO PROBABILITY

Probability measures the likelihood of an event occurring.

Probability ranges from 0 to 1.

0 represents an impossible event.
1 represents a certain event.

For equally likely outcomes:

P(A) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of outcomes.

34. BASIC PROBABILITY TERMS

Experiment:
A process that produces an outcome.

Sample space:
The set of all possible outcomes.

Event:
A subset of the sample space.

Favourable outcomes:
Outcomes that satisfy the event.

Complement:
The event that A does not occur.

Notation:
P(A') = 1 - P(A).

35. PROBABILITY RULES

Addition rule:

P(A union B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A intersection B).

If A and B are mutually exclusive:

P(A union B) = P(A) + P(B).

Multiplication rule:

P(A intersection B) = P(A)P(B|A).

If A and B are independent:

P(A intersection B) = P(A)P(B).

36. CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event has already occurred.

P(A|B) = P(A intersection B)/P(B), provided P(B) > 0.

Conditional probability is useful when the occurrence of one event changes the probability of another event.

37. BAYES' THEOREM

Bayes' theorem calculates the probability of a hypothesis given observed evidence.

P(A|B) = [P(B|A)P(A)]/P(B), provided P(B) > 0.

Where:
P(A) is the prior probability.
P(B|A) is the likelihood.
P(A|B) is the posterior probability.

Applications:
- Medical testing.
- Spam filtering.
- Fault diagnosis.
- Machine learning classification.

38. RANDOM VARIABLES

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.

Types:

Discrete random variable:
Takes a finite or countably infinite set of values.

Continuous random variable:
Takes values over a continuous range.

Examples:
- Number of heads in coin tosses is discrete.
- Time required to complete a task is continuous.

39. PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTIONS

A probability distribution describes the possible values of a random variable and their probabilities.

Discrete probability distributions:
- Bernoulli distribution.
- Binomial distribution.
- Poisson distribution.

Continuous probability distributions:
- Uniform distribution.
- Normal distribution.
- Exponential distribution.

40. EXPECTATION AND VARIANCE

Expected value:
The long-run average value of a random variable.

For a discrete random variable:

E(X) = Sum of x * P(X=x).

Variance:
Measures the spread of a random variable around its expected value.

Var(X) = E[(X - E(X))^2].

Also:

Var(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2.

Standard deviation:

SD(X) = sqrt(Var(X)).

41. STATISTICS

Statistics is the branch of mathematics concerned with collecting, organizing, analyzing, interpreting, and presenting data.

Two major branches:
- Descriptive statistics.
- Inferential statistics.

Descriptive statistics summarizes observed data.

Inferential statistics uses sample data to draw conclusions about a population.

42. MEASURES OF CENTRAL TENDENCY

Mean:
The arithmetic average.

Mean = Sum of observations / Number of observations.

Median:
The middle value after arranging observations in order.

For an even number of observations, the median is the average of the two middle values.

Mode:
The most frequently occurring value.

43. MEASURES OF DISPERSION

Range:
Difference between maximum and minimum values.

Variance:
Average squared deviation from the mean, with the appropriate population or sample denominator.

Population variance:

sigma^2 = Sum((x - mu)^2)/N.

Sample variance:

s^2 = Sum((x - x_bar)^2)/(n - 1).

Standard deviation:
Square root of variance.

Interquartile range:
Difference between the third quartile and first quartile.

IQR = Q3 - Q1.

44. CORRELATION

Correlation measures the strength and direction of association between two variables.

The Pearson correlation coefficient is represented by r.

Its value ranges from -1 to +1.

r = +1 indicates perfect positive linear correlation.

r = -1 indicates perfect negative linear correlation.

r = 0 indicates no linear correlation.

Correlation does not necessarily imply causation.

45. REGRESSION

Regression models the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables.

Simple linear regression:

y = mx + c.

Where:
- y is the dependent variable.
- x is the independent variable.
- m is the slope.
- c is the intercept.

Applications:
- Price prediction.
- Demand forecasting.
- Trend analysis.
- Scientific modelling.

46. DISCRETE MATHEMATICS

Discrete mathematics studies mathematical structures that are fundamentally discrete rather than continuous.

It is important in computer science.

Topics include:
- Sets.
- Logic.
- Relations.
- Functions.
- Combinatorics.
- Graph theory.
- Trees.
- Boolean algebra.
- Number theory.

47. SET THEORY

A set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects.

Example:
A = {1, 2, 3, 4}.

Types:
- Empty set.
- Finite set.
- Infinite set.
- Universal set.
- Equal sets.
- Subsets.
- Disjoint sets.

Operations:

Union:
A union B contains elements belonging to A or B or both.

Intersection:
A intersection B contains elements common to both sets.

Difference:
A - B contains elements belonging to A but not B.

Complement:
A' contains elements of the universal set that do not belong to A.

48. SET IDENTITIES

Commutative laws:

A union B = B union A.

A intersection B = B intersection A.

Associative laws:

(A union B) union C = A union (B union C).

(A intersection B) intersection C = A intersection (B intersection C).

Distributive laws:

A intersection (B union C) = (A intersection B) union (A intersection C).

A union (B intersection C) = (A union B) intersection (A union C).

De Morgan's laws:

(A union B)' = A' intersection B'.

(A intersection B)' = A' union B'.

49. MATHEMATICAL LOGIC

Mathematical logic studies statements and reasoning.

A proposition is a declarative statement that is either true or false.

Logical operators:

AND:
True only when both propositions are true.

OR:
True when at least one proposition is true.

NOT:
Reverses the truth value.

Implication:
p -> q is false only when p is true and q is false.

Biconditional:
p <-> q is true when p and q have the same truth value.

50. TRUTH TABLES

A truth table lists the truth values of a logical expression for all possible input combinations.

For two propositions p and q:

p q | p AND q | p OR q
T T | T | T
T F | F | T
F T | F | T
F F | F | F

A tautology is a proposition that is always true.

A contradiction is a proposition that is always false.

A contingency is a proposition that is true for some assignments and false for others.

51. RELATIONS

A relation from set A to set B is a subset of the Cartesian product A x B.

A relation on set A is a subset of A x A.

Properties:

Reflexive:
Every element is related to itself.

Symmetric:
If a is related to b, then b is related to a.

Antisymmetric:
If a is related to b and b is related to a, then a = b.

Transitive:
If a is related to b and b is related to c, then a is related to c.

52. EQUIVALENCE RELATIONS AND PARTIAL ORDERS

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive.

A set together with a partial order forms a partially ordered set or poset.

Examples of partial orders include subset inclusion and divisibility on positive integers.

53. COMBINATORICS

Combinatorics studies counting, arrangement, and selection.

Factorial:

n! = n * (n-1) * ... * 2 * 1.

Also:

0! = 1.

Permutation:
An arrangement in which order matters.

nPr = n!/(n-r)!.

Combination:
A selection in which order does not matter.

nCr = n!/[r!(n-r)!].

54. GRAPH THEORY

A graph consists of vertices and edges connecting pairs of vertices.

Notation:
G = (V, E).

Where:
V is the set of vertices.
E is the set of edges.

Types:
- Undirected graph.
- Directed graph.
- Weighted graph.
- Complete graph.
- Connected graph.
- Bipartite graph.

Degree:
The number of edges incident to a vertex in an undirected graph.

Path:
A sequence of vertices connected by edges.

Cycle:
A path that begins and ends at the same vertex, with the required distinctness conditions depending on the graph definition.

55. TREES

A tree is a connected undirected graph with no cycles.

Properties:
- A tree with n vertices has n - 1 edges.
- There is exactly one simple path between every pair of vertices.
- Removing any edge disconnects a tree.
- Adding an edge between two vertices creates a cycle.

Binary tree:
A tree in which each node has at most two children.

Binary Search Tree:
A binary tree in which values in the left subtree are ordered below the node and values in the right subtree are ordered above the node, according to the chosen ordering rule.

56. BOOLEAN ALGEBRA

Boolean algebra operates on binary values 0 and 1.

Basic operations:
- AND.
- OR.
- NOT.

Important laws:

Identity:
A AND 1 = A.
A OR 0 = A.

Domination:
A AND 0 = 0.
A OR 1 = 1.

Idempotent:
A AND A = A.
A OR A = A.

Complement:
A AND NOT A = 0.
A OR NOT A = 1.

Double negation:
NOT(NOT A) = A.

De Morgan's laws:
NOT(A AND B) = NOT A OR NOT B.
NOT(A OR B) = NOT A AND NOT B.

57. OPTIMIZATION

Optimization is the process of finding the best solution according to an objective function and constraints.

An optimization problem generally includes:
- Decision variables.
- Objective function.
- Constraints.
- Feasible region.

The objective may be to maximize or minimize a function.

Examples:
- Minimize production cost.
- Maximize profit.
- Minimize network latency.
- Optimize machine learning parameters.

58. LINEAR PROGRAMMING

Linear programming optimizes a linear objective function subject to linear constraints.

General form:

Maximize or minimize:

Z = c1x1 + c2x2 + ... + cnxn.

Subject to linear constraints.

Decision variables must satisfy the specified restrictions, such as non-negativity.

Methods:
- Graphical method.
- Simplex method.

The graphical method is commonly used for problems with two decision variables.

59. GRADIENT DESCENT

Gradient descent is an iterative optimization algorithm used to minimize a differentiable objective function.

Update rule:

theta_new = theta_old - learning_rate * gradient.

The learning rate controls the step size.

Types:
- Batch gradient descent.
- Stochastic gradient descent.
- Mini-batch gradient descent.

Gradient descent is widely used to train machine learning and neural network models.

### DOMAIN: SCIENCE

﻿SCIENCE FUNDAMENTALS

1. INTRODUCTION TO SCIENCE

Science is the systematic study of the natural world through observation, experimentation, measurement, and reasoning.

Major branches:
- Physics: Studies matter, energy, motion, and forces.
- Chemistry: Studies matter, its composition, properties, and transformations.
- Biology: Studies living organisms and life processes.
- Earth Science: Studies the Earth and its systems.
- Astronomy: Studies celestial objects and the universe.

2. SCIENTIFIC METHOD

The scientific method is a systematic approach used to investigate questions and develop explanations.

Steps:
1. Observation: Identify a phenomenon.
2. Question: Formulate a question about the observation.
3. Hypothesis: Propose a testable explanation.
4. Experiment: Design and perform an investigation.
5. Data collection: Record observations and measurements.
6. Analysis: Interpret the collected data.
7. Conclusion: Determine whether the evidence supports the hypothesis.
8. Communication: Share findings for review and further investigation.

A scientific hypothesis must be testable and capable of being evaluated using evidence.

3. MEASUREMENT AND SI UNITS

Measurement assigns numerical values and units to physical quantities.

The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units:

Length: metre (m).
Mass: kilogram (kg).
Time: second (s).
Electric current: ampere (A).
Temperature: kelvin (K).
Amount of substance: mole (mol).
Luminous intensity: candela (cd).

Derived units:
- Velocity: metre per second (m/s).
- Acceleration: metre per second squared (m/s^2).
- Force: newton (N).
- Energy: joule (J).
- Power: watt (W).
- Pressure: pascal (Pa).

4. SCALARS AND VECTORS

A scalar quantity has magnitude only.

Examples:
- Mass.
- Time.
- Temperature.
- Energy.
- Distance.

A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction.

Examples:
- Displacement.
- Velocity.
- Acceleration.
- Force.
- Momentum.

5. MATTER

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Common states of matter:
- Solid: Has a definite shape and volume.
- Liquid: Has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container.
- Gas: Has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume.
- Plasma: An ionized state of matter containing charged particles.

Changes of state:
- Melting: Solid to liquid.
- Freezing: Liquid to solid.
- Vaporization: Liquid to gas.
- Condensation: Gas to liquid.
- Sublimation: Solid directly to gas.
- Deposition: Gas directly to solid.

6. MOTION

Motion is the change in position of an object with respect to a reference point over time.

Distance:
The total length of the path travelled. It is a scalar quantity.

Displacement:
The change in position from initial to final point. It is a vector quantity.

Speed:
Rate of change of distance.

Speed = Distance / Time.

Velocity:
Rate of change of displacement.

Average velocity = Displacement / Time taken.

Acceleration:
Rate of change of velocity.

Average acceleration = Change in velocity / Time taken.

7. TYPES OF MOTION

Linear motion:
Movement along a straight line.

Circular motion:
Movement along a circular path.

Rotational motion:
Movement of an object around an axis.

Oscillatory motion:
Repeated movement around an equilibrium position.

Periodic motion:
Motion that repeats after equal intervals of time.

8. NEWTON'S LAWS OF MOTION

First Law - Law of Inertia:
An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

Second Law:
The net force acting on an object equals the rate of change of its momentum.

For constant mass:

F = ma.

Where:
F = Net force.
m = Mass.
a = Acceleration.

Third Law:
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

The action and reaction forces act on different bodies.

9. FORCE

Force is an interaction that can change the motion or shape of an object.

SI unit: Newton (N).

Types:
- Gravitational force.
- Frictional force.
- Normal force.
- Tension.
- Magnetic force.
- Electrostatic force.

Balanced forces:
Net force is zero.

Unbalanced forces:
Net force is non-zero and can change an object's velocity.

10. FRICTION

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion between surfaces in contact.

Types:
- Static friction.
- Sliding friction.
- Rolling friction.
- Fluid friction.

Friction can:
- Help us walk.
- Allow vehicles to brake.
- Produce heat.
- Cause mechanical wear.

11. WORK, ENERGY, AND POWER

Work is done when a force causes displacement.

For a constant force:

W = F s cos(theta).

Where:
F = Force.
s = Displacement.
theta = Angle between force and displacement.

SI unit: Joule (J).

Energy is the capacity to do work.

Forms of energy:
- Kinetic energy.
- Potential energy.
- Thermal energy.
- Chemical energy.
- Electrical energy.
- Nuclear energy.
- Light energy.

Kinetic energy:

KE = 1/2 mv^2.

Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface:

PE = mgh.

Power is the rate of doing work.

Power = Work / Time.

SI unit: Watt (W).

12. LAW OF CONSERVATION OF ENERGY

Energy cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system. It can only be transferred or transformed from one form to another.

Example:
In a falling object, gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases, ignoring energy losses.

13. MOMENTUM

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity.

p = mv.

SI unit: kg m/s.

Momentum is a vector quantity.

Impulse:
Impulse equals the change in momentum.

J = F * delta(t), for constant force.

In an isolated system, total momentum remains constant if the net external force is zero.

14. GRAVITATION

Gravitation is the attractive force between objects with mass.

Newton's law of universal gravitation:

F = G(m1*m2)/r^2.

Where:
G = Universal gravitational constant.
m1 and m2 = Masses of objects.
r = Distance between their centres.

Acceleration due to gravity near Earth's surface is approximately 9.8 m/s^2.

15. PRESSURE

Pressure is force acting per unit area.

P = F/A.

SI unit: Pascal (Pa).

Pressure in a fluid at rest increases with depth.

For a fluid of density rho at depth h:

P = rho*g*h.

This is gauge pressure due to the fluid column.

16. HEAT AND TEMPERATURE

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Temperature measures the thermal state of a system.

Heat transfer methods:

Conduction:
Transfer of thermal energy through direct microscopic interactions, commonly important in solids.

Convection:
Transfer of thermal energy through the bulk movement of fluids.

Radiation:
Transfer of energy through electromagnetic waves and does not require a material medium.

17. SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY

Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin.

Q = mc(delta T).

Where:
Q = Heat transferred.
m = Mass.
c = Specific heat capacity.
delta T = Change in temperature.

SI unit of specific heat capacity: J/(kg K).

18. THERMODYNAMICS

Thermodynamics studies heat, work, temperature, and energy transformations.

Zeroth Law:
If two systems are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

First Law:
Energy is conserved.

Using the convention that W is work done by the system:

delta U = Q - W.

Second Law:
The entropy of an isolated system does not decrease in a spontaneous process.

Third Law:
The entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as its temperature approaches absolute zero.

19. WAVES

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one location to another.

Mechanical waves require a material medium.

Electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum.

Types:
- Transverse waves.
- Longitudinal waves.

Transverse wave:
Particle oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.

Longitudinal wave:
Particle oscillations are parallel to the direction of wave propagation.

20. WAVE PARAMETERS

Wavelength:
Distance between two successive points in the same phase, such as consecutive crests.

Frequency:
Number of complete cycles per second.

SI unit: Hertz (Hz).

Time period:
Time required for one complete cycle.

T = 1/f.

Wave speed:

v = f(lambda).

Where lambda represents wavelength.

Amplitude:
Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.

21. SOUND

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating objects.

In air, sound generally travels as a longitudinal wave.

Properties:
- Frequency determines perceived pitch.
- Amplitude is related to sound intensity.
- Timbre helps distinguish sound sources.

Sound requires a material medium and cannot travel through a vacuum.

The speed of sound depends on the properties and conditions of the medium.

22. LIGHT

Light is electromagnetic radiation visible to the human eye.

Properties:
- Travels through a vacuum.
- Undergoes reflection and refraction.
- Exhibits diffraction and interference.
- Can be polarized.

Speed of light in vacuum:

c approximately equals 3 * 10^8 m/s.

23. REFLECTION OF LIGHT

Reflection occurs when light returns into the original medium after striking a surface.

Laws:
1. The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
2. Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.

Applications:
- Mirrors.
- Periscopes.
- Optical instruments.

24. REFRACTION OF LIGHT

Refraction is the change in direction of light when it passes between media with different refractive indices.

Snell's law:

n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2).

Refractive index:

n = c/v.

Where:
c = Speed of light in vacuum.
v = Speed of light in the medium.

Applications:
- Lenses.
- Spectacles.
- Cameras.
- Microscopes.

25. ELECTRICITY

Electricity involves electric charge and its effects.

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter.

Two types:
- Positive charge.
- Negative charge.

Like charges repel.
Unlike charges attract.

SI unit of electric charge: Coulomb (C).

26. ELECTRIC CURRENT

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.

I = Q/t.

SI unit: Ampere (A).

Conventional current direction is defined as the direction positive charges would move.

27. VOLTAGE AND RESISTANCE

Voltage is the electric potential difference between two points.

SI unit: Volt (V).

Resistance is the opposition to electric current.

SI unit: Ohm (ohm symbol).

28. OHM'S LAW

Ohm's law states that for a conductor under constant physical conditions, current is directly proportional to voltage.

V = IR.

Where:
V = Voltage.
I = Current.
R = Resistance.

29. ELECTRIC POWER AND ENERGY

Electric power is the rate of electrical energy transfer.

P = VI.

For a resistive element:

P = I^2 R.

Also:

P = V^2/R.

Electrical energy:

E = Pt.

Commercial electrical energy is commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

30. ELECTRIC CIRCUITS

A basic electric circuit contains:
- Source.
- Conducting wires.
- Load.
- Switching or control element.

Series circuit:
Components are connected along a single current path.

Parallel circuit:
Components are connected across common nodes, providing multiple current paths.

For resistors in series:

R_total = R1 + R2 + ... + Rn.

For resistors in parallel:

1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... + 1/Rn.

31. MAGNETISM

Magnetism is associated with moving electric charges and magnetic moments.

A magnet has north and south poles.

Like magnetic poles repel.
Unlike magnetic poles attract.

Magnetic fields describe the magnetic influence in a region.

SI unit of magnetic flux density: Tesla (T).

32. ELECTROMAGNETISM

Electromagnetism studies the relationship between electric and magnetic fields.

An electric current can produce a magnetic field.

A changing magnetic flux can induce an electromotive force.

Faraday's law describes electromagnetic induction.

Lenz's law states that the induced current produces an effect that opposes the change in magnetic flux that caused it.

Applications:
- Electric motors.
- Generators.
- Transformers.
- Electromagnets.

33. ATOMIC STRUCTURE

An atom is the basic unit of a chemical element that retains its chemical identity.

Main subatomic particles:

Proton:
Positive charge; located in the nucleus.

Neutron:
No electric charge; located in the nucleus.

Electron:
Negative charge; occupies regions around the nucleus.

Atomic number:
Number of protons in an atom's nucleus.

Mass number:
Total number of protons and neutrons.

In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

34. ISOTOPES AND IONS

Isotopes:
Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Ions:
Electrically charged atoms or groups of atoms.

Cation:
A positively charged ion formed by losing electrons.

Anion:
A negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons.

35. PERIODIC TABLE

The periodic table organizes chemical elements according to increasing atomic number.

Rows are called periods.

Columns are called groups.

Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because of related valence electron configurations.

Broad categories:
- Metals.
- Non-metals.
- Metalloids.

36. CHEMICAL BONDING

Chemical bonds hold atoms or ions together.

Ionic bond:
Forms through electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, commonly following electron transfer.

Covalent bond:
Forms when atoms share electron pairs.

Metallic bond:
Involves attraction between positive metal ion cores and delocalized electrons.

37. CHEMICAL REACTIONS

A chemical reaction converts reactants into products through the rearrangement of atoms.

Reactants:
Starting substances.

Products:
Substances formed.

Common types:
- Combination reaction.
- Decomposition reaction.
- Displacement reaction.
- Double displacement reaction.
- Combustion reaction.
- Redox reaction.

A balanced chemical equation has equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides.

38. ACIDS, BASES, AND SALTS

Acid:
A substance that donates protons in the Brønsted-Lowry definition.

Base:
A substance that accepts protons in the Brønsted-Lowry definition.

Salt:
An ionic compound formed through reactions involving acids and bases or other chemical processes.

pH:
A measure related to hydrogen ion activity.

At approximately 25 degrees Celsius in dilute aqueous solutions:
- pH below 7 is acidic.
- pH equal to 7 is neutral.
- pH above 7 is basic.

39. CHEMICAL STOICHIOMETRY

Stoichiometry studies quantitative relationships between reactants and products in chemical reactions.

A mole represents a specified amount of substance.

Avogadro constant:

6.02214076 * 10^23 entities per mole.

Molar mass:
Mass of one mole of a substance.

Balanced chemical equations provide mole ratios for calculations.

40. INTRODUCTION TO BIOLOGY

Biology is the scientific study of living organisms and life processes.

Major branches:
- Botany.
- Zoology.
- Microbiology.
- Genetics.
- Ecology.
- Molecular biology.
- Biotechnology.

Characteristics commonly associated with living organisms include cellular organization, metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, and evolution across populations.

41. CELL THEORY

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Cell theory:
1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function.
3. New cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Two broad cell types:
- Prokaryotic cells.
- Eukaryotic cells.

42. PROKARYOTIC AND EUKARYOTIC CELLS

Prokaryotic cells:
Do not have a membrane-bound nucleus. Their genetic material is located in a nucleoid region.

Examples include bacteria and archaea.

Eukaryotic cells:
Have a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Examples include animal, plant, fungal, and protist cells.

43. CELL ORGANELLES

Nucleus:
Contains most of the cell's genetic material and regulates gene expression.

Mitochondria:
Carry out important stages of cellular respiration and produce much of the cell's ATP.

Ribosomes:
Synthesize proteins.

Endoplasmic reticulum:
Involved in protein and lipid synthesis and processing.

Golgi apparatus:
Modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids.

Lysosomes:
Contain enzymes that help break down cellular materials.

Chloroplasts:
In plants and algae, carry out photosynthesis.

Cell membrane:
Controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.

44. DNA AND RNA

DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid):
Stores hereditary information in most organisms.

RNA (Ribonucleic Acid):
Participates in gene expression and protein synthesis and performs other cellular roles.

DNA generally contains:
- Adenine.
- Thymine.
- Guanine.
- Cytosine.

RNA generally contains:
- Adenine.
- Uracil.
- Guanine.
- Cytosine.

45. GENETICS

Genetics is the study of heredity and variation.

Gene:
A segment of genetic material associated with a functional product.

Allele:
An alternative form of a gene.

Genotype:
The genetic constitution of an organism for a particular trait or set of traits.

Phenotype:
Observable characteristics resulting from genetic and environmental influences.

Dominant allele:
An allele that can determine a phenotype in a heterozygote under a simple dominance model.

Recessive allele:
An allele whose associated phenotype is masked in a heterozygote under a simple dominance model.

46. MENDEL'S LAWS OF INHERITANCE

Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants helped establish fundamental principles of inheritance.

Law of Segregation:
The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives one allele.

Law of Independent Assortment:
Alleles of different genes assort independently when the genes are unlinked or sufficiently far apart, subject to genetic linkage.

Law of Dominance:
In simple complete-dominance cases, one allele can mask the expression of another in a heterozygote.

47. PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy.

In oxygenic photosynthesis, carbon dioxide and water are used to produce carbohydrates, and oxygen is released.

Simplified equation:

6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6O2.

In plants, photosynthesis mainly occurs in chloroplasts.

48. CELLULAR RESPIRATION

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells extract energy from nutrients and use it to produce ATP.

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain.

A simplified overall equation for glucose oxidation is:

C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy.

Major stages:
- Glycolysis.
- Pyruvate oxidation.
- Citric acid cycle.
- Oxidative phosphorylation.

49. HUMAN BODY SYSTEMS

Digestive system:
Breaks down food and absorbs nutrients.

Respiratory system:
Supports gas exchange, taking in oxygen and removing carbon dioxide.

Circulatory system:
Transports blood, oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.

Nervous system:
Coordinates rapid communication and responses using electrical and chemical signals.

Skeletal system:
Supports the body, protects organs, and helps movement.

Muscular system:
Produces movement and helps maintain posture.

Excretory system:
Removes metabolic waste and regulates water and electrolyte balance.

Endocrine system:
Uses hormones to regulate body functions.

Immune system:
Defends the body against pathogens and other harmful substances.

50. ECOLOGY

Ecology studies interactions between organisms and their environment.

Levels of ecological organization:
- Organism.
- Population.
- Community.
- Ecosystem.
- Biome.
- Biosphere.

Ecosystem:
A system consisting of living organisms and their physical environment interacting together.

Biotic factors:
Living components.

Abiotic factors:
Non-living environmental components such as water, light, soil, and temperature.

51. FOOD CHAINS AND FOOD WEBS

Food chain:
A linear sequence showing the transfer of energy and nutrients between organisms.

Producer:
An organism that produces organic matter, commonly through photosynthesis.

Consumer:
An organism that obtains energy by consuming other organisms or organic matter.

Decomposer:
An organism that breaks down dead organic matter and recycles nutrients.

Food web:
A network of interconnected food chains.

Energy generally decreases at successive trophic levels because organisms use energy for life processes and lose much of it as heat.

52. BIODIVERSITY

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels.

Importance:
- Maintains ecosystem functioning.
- Provides food and resources.
- Supports pollination and nutrient cycling.
- Contributes to ecosystem resilience.
- Has cultural, scientific, and economic value.

Threats:
- Habitat destruction.
- Pollution.
- Climate change.
- Invasive species.
- Overexploitation.

53. ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE

Environmental science studies interactions between natural systems and human activities.

Major environmental issues:
- Climate change.
- Air pollution.
- Water pollution.
- Soil degradation.
- Deforestation.
- Biodiversity loss.
- Waste management.

Sustainable development aims to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

54. CLIMATE CHANGE

Climate change refers to long-term changes in temperature and weather patterns.

Human activities, especially greenhouse gas emissions from burning fossil fuels and land-use changes, are major drivers of recent global warming.

Important greenhouse gases:
- Carbon dioxide.
- Methane.
- Nitrous oxide.
- Certain industrial gases.

Potential effects:
- Rising average temperatures.
- Sea-level rise.
- Changes in rainfall patterns.
- More frequent or intense heat extremes in many regions.
- Ecosystem disruption.

Question: What is a database?
Answer: A database is an organized collection of information that can be stored, managed, retrieved, and updated efficiently. For example, a college database stores student names, roll numbers, courses, and marks.

Question: Why do we use databases?
Answer: Databases help applications store and manage large amounts of information. They support searching, updating, deleting, and organizing records. They also help maintain data consistency and reduce unnecessary duplication.

Question: What is a primary key?
Answer: A primary key is a column or combination of columns that uniquely identifies every row in a database table. For example, StudentID can uniquely identify each student in a student table.

Question: What is a foreign key?
Answer: A foreign key is a column that refers to a key in another table. It establishes relationships between tables. For example, a DepartmentID in an Employee table can refer to DepartmentID in a Department table.

Question: Explain the difference between a stack and a queue.
Answer: A stack follows Last In First Out, also called LIFO. The last inserted element is removed first. A queue follows First In First Out, also called FIFO. The first inserted element is removed first. A stack is similar to a pile of plates, while a queue is similar to people waiting in a line.

Question: What is inheritance in Java?
Answer: Inheritance is an object-oriented programming feature in which one class acquires the properties and methods of another class. The existing class is called the parent class, and the new class is called the child class. It promotes code reuse.

Question: Give an example of inheritance in Java.
Answer: Consider a class Animal containing a method eat(). A class Dog can extend Animal and inherit the eat() method. The Dog class can also define its own method bark(). In Java, inheritance can be implemented using the extends keyword.

Question: What is polymorphism?
Answer: Polymorphism means one interface can represent different forms of behaviour. In Java, method overloading and method overriding are two ways to achieve polymorphism. It makes programs more flexible and reusable.

Question: What is an algorithm?
Answer: An algorithm is a finite sequence of well-defined instructions used to solve a problem. An algorithm accepts input, performs operations, and produces an output.

Question: Explain binary search.
Answer: Binary search is a searching algorithm used on sorted data. It compares the target value with the middle element. If the target is smaller, it searches the left half. If the target is larger, it searches the right half. This process continues until the element is found or the search range becomes empty.

Question: What is an operating system?
Answer: An operating system is system software that manages computer hardware and software resources. It manages processes, memory, files, and input and output devices. Examples include Windows and Linux.

Question: What is a computer network?
Answer: A computer network is a collection of interconnected devices that communicate and exchange information. Networks allow devices to share files, resources, and services.

Question: What is a Transformer?
Answer: A Transformer is a neural network architecture that uses attention mechanisms to process relationships between tokens in a sequence. Transformers are widely used in language modelling because they can learn contextual relationships between words and tokens.

Question: What is self-attention?
Answer: Self-attention is a mechanism that allows each token to consider information from other tokens in the same sequence. It helps a Transformer identify relationships between different parts of the input.

Question: What is a language model?
Answer: A language model is a machine learning model that learns statistical patterns in sequences of tokens. It uses these patterns to predict the next token and generate text.
