ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

1. INTRODUCTION TO ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science concerned with creating systems that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence.

These tasks include:
- Learning from experience.
- Reasoning and problem-solving.
- Understanding language.
- Recognizing images and patterns.
- Making decisions.
- Planning and prediction.

Examples of AI applications include virtual assistants, recommendation systems, fraud detection, autonomous vehicles, and medical image analysis.

2. GOALS OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

The major goals of AI are:
- Develop systems that can learn from data.
- Enable machines to reason and solve problems.
- Understand and process human language.
- Recognize objects, speech, and patterns.
- Automate repetitive and complex tasks.
- Support intelligent decision-making.

3. HISTORY OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

1950:
Alan Turing proposed the imitation game, commonly known as the Turing Test.

1956:
The term Artificial Intelligence was introduced at the Dartmouth Conference.

1960s and 1970s:
Early AI research focused on symbolic reasoning, problem-solving, and expert systems.

1980s:
Expert systems became popular in commercial applications.

1990s:
Machine learning approaches gained importance.

2010s:
Deep learning made major progress in image recognition, speech recognition, and language processing.

2020s:
Generative AI and large language models became widely used for text, image, audio, and code generation.

4. TYPES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BASED ON CAPABILITY

Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI):
AI designed to perform specific tasks. Examples include spam filters, recommendation systems, and voice assistants.

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):
A hypothetical form of AI that can understand and perform a broad range of intellectual tasks at a human-like level.

Artificial Superintelligence (ASI):
A hypothetical form of intelligence that would significantly exceed human intellectual capabilities.

Modern deployed AI systems are generally examples of narrow AI.

5. TYPES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BASED ON FUNCTIONALITY

Reactive Machines:
Systems that respond to current inputs without storing experiences for future decisions.

Limited Memory AI:
Systems that use information from past observations or training to make decisions.

Theory of Mind AI:
A proposed AI capability involving understanding human emotions, beliefs, and intentions.

Self-Aware AI:
A hypothetical AI system with consciousness and self-awareness.

6. INTELLIGENT AGENTS

An intelligent agent is a system that perceives its environment through sensors and takes actions through actuators to achieve goals.

Agent components:
- Sensors: Collect information from the environment.
- Percepts: Information received by the agent.
- Decision mechanism: Determines what action to take.
- Actuators: Perform actions in the environment.

Agent types:
- Simple reflex agent.
- Model-based reflex agent.
- Goal-based agent.
- Utility-based agent.
- Learning agent.

A rational agent selects actions that maximize expected performance based on its available information and objectives.

7. PROBLEM-SOLVING IN AI

AI problem-solving involves finding a sequence of actions that transforms an initial state into a desired goal state.

Important components:
- Initial state.
- Goal state.
- Actions.
- Transition model.
- Path cost.

Search algorithms:

Breadth-First Search (BFS):
Explores nodes level by level. It is complete for finite branching factors and finds a shortest path when all step costs are equal.

Depth-First Search (DFS):
Explores one branch deeply before backtracking. It uses less memory in many cases but does not generally guarantee the shortest path.

Uniform Cost Search:
Expands the node with the lowest accumulated path cost. It is optimal under suitable non-negative cost assumptions.

Greedy Best-First Search:
Selects nodes using a heuristic estimate of distance to the goal. It can be fast but is not generally optimal.

A* Search:
Uses both accumulated path cost and estimated remaining cost.

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

Where:
g(n) = Cost from the initial state to node n.
h(n) = Estimated cost from node n to the goal.

A* is optimal under suitable conditions, such as an admissible heuristic and appropriate graph-search handling.

8. KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

Knowledge representation is the method of storing facts, concepts, and relationships so an AI system can reason with them.

Common techniques:
- Logical representation.
- Semantic networks.
- Frames.
- Production rules.
- Ontologies.

Example:
Rule: If a person has a fever and cough, they may have a respiratory infection.
This is a simplified rule and does not establish a medical diagnosis.

9. MACHINE LEARNING

Machine Learning (ML) is a subfield of AI that enables systems to learn patterns from data and improve their performance without being explicitly programmed with every rule.

A machine learning workflow:
1. Define the problem.
2. Collect data.
3. Clean and preprocess data.
4. Select relevant features.
5. Split data into training, validation, and test sets.
6. Select a model.
7. Train the model.
8. Evaluate performance.
9. Tune parameters.
10. Deploy and monitor the model.

10. TYPES OF MACHINE LEARNING

Supervised Learning:
The model learns from labelled training data containing input-output pairs.

Common tasks:
- Classification: Predicts categories.
- Regression: Predicts continuous numerical values.

Examples:
- Email spam detection.
- House price prediction.
- Student performance prediction.

Unsupervised Learning:
The model discovers patterns in unlabelled data.

Common tasks:
- Clustering.
- Dimensionality reduction.
- Association rule mining.

Examples:
- Customer segmentation.
- Grouping similar documents.

Semi-Supervised Learning:
Uses a small amount of labelled data together with a larger amount of unlabelled data.

Reinforcement Learning:
An agent learns by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties.

11. SUPERVISED LEARNING ALGORITHMS

Linear Regression:
Predicts a continuous value using a linear relationship between input features and the target.

Logistic Regression:
A classification algorithm that estimates class probabilities using a logistic function in binary classification.

Decision Tree:
Uses a tree-like structure of decisions to make predictions.

Random Forest:
Combines predictions from multiple decision trees to improve robustness and predictive performance.

K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN):
Predicts the class or value of a data point using nearby training examples.

Support Vector Machine (SVM):
Finds a decision boundary that separates classes with a large margin.

Naive Bayes:
Uses Bayes' theorem with a conditional independence assumption between features.

12. UNSUPERVISED LEARNING ALGORITHMS

K-Means Clustering:
Partitions data into K clusters by assigning each point to the nearest cluster centroid and updating the centroids.

Hierarchical Clustering:
Builds a hierarchy of clusters by repeatedly merging or splitting groups.

DBSCAN:
Groups closely packed data points and identifies noise using density-based rules.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA):
A dimensionality-reduction technique that transforms features into orthogonal components capturing decreasing amounts of variance.

13. REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a learning approach in which an agent interacts with an environment and learns actions that maximize cumulative reward.

Main components:
- Agent: Learner or decision-maker.
- Environment: The world in which the agent operates.
- State: Current situation.
- Action: Decision taken by the agent.
- Reward: Feedback received after an action.
- Policy: Strategy used to select actions.

The agent aims to maximize expected cumulative reward.

Important concepts:
- Exploration: Trying actions to discover their outcomes.
- Exploitation: Choosing actions based on current knowledge.
- Discount factor: Determines the importance of future rewards.

14. DEEP LEARNING

Deep Learning is a subfield of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn complex patterns from data.

Applications:
- Image recognition.
- Speech recognition.
- Language translation.
- Object detection.
- Text generation.
- Medical image analysis.

Deep learning generally requires substantial training data and computational resources.

15. ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORKS

An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is a computational model inspired by the interconnected structure of biological neurons.

Main components:
- Input layer: Receives input features.
- Hidden layers: Learn intermediate representations.
- Output layer: Produces predictions.

A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies an activation function.

z = w1x1 + w2x2 + ... + wnxn + b

Output = Activation(z)

Where:
- x represents input values.
- w represents weights.
- b represents bias.
- Activation introduces non-linearity.

16. ACTIVATION FUNCTIONS

Activation functions introduce non-linearity into neural networks.

Sigmoid:
Maps an input to a value between 0 and 1.

ReLU:
Returns zero for negative inputs and the input itself for positive inputs.

ReLU(x) = max(0, x)

Tanh:
Maps inputs to values between -1 and 1.

Softmax:
Converts a vector of values into a probability distribution.

17. FORWARD PROPAGATION

Forward propagation is the process of passing input data through a neural network to calculate its output.

Steps:
1. Input data enters the input layer.
2. Each layer calculates weighted sums and biases.
3. Activation functions are applied.
4. The result is passed to the next layer.
5. The output layer generates the prediction.

18. BACKPROPAGATION

Backpropagation is an algorithm used to calculate gradients of a neural network's loss function with respect to its parameters.

Steps:
1. Perform forward propagation.
2. Calculate prediction error using a loss function.
3. Compute gradients using the chain rule.
4. Propagate gradient information backward through the network.
5. Use an optimizer to update weights and biases.

Backpropagation computes gradients; an optimizer such as gradient descent or Adam uses them to update parameters.

19. LOSS FUNCTIONS

A loss function measures the difference between model predictions and actual target values.

Mean Squared Error (MSE):
Commonly used for regression.

MSE = (1/n) * sum((actual - predicted)^2)

Mean Absolute Error (MAE):
Calculates the average absolute prediction error.

Binary Cross-Entropy:
Commonly used for binary classification.

Categorical Cross-Entropy:
Commonly used for multi-class classification.

The training process aims to minimize the selected loss function.

20. OPTIMIZATION ALGORITHMS

Gradient Descent:
Updates model parameters in the direction opposite to the loss gradient.

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD):
Updates parameters using one training example or a small sample at a time.

Mini-Batch Gradient Descent:
Updates parameters using a small batch of training examples.

Adam:
An adaptive optimization algorithm that maintains moving averages of gradients and squared gradients.

Learning Rate:
Controls the size of parameter updates during training.

21. CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a neural network architecture commonly used for image and spatial data.

Main components:
- Convolutional layer: Extracts local patterns using filters.
- Activation layer: Introduces non-linearity.
- Pooling layer: Reduces spatial dimensions.
- Fully connected layer: Produces final predictions.

Applications:
- Image classification.
- Face recognition.
- Object detection.
- Medical image analysis.

22. RECURRENT NEURAL NETWORKS

A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) is designed to process sequential data by maintaining information from previous time steps.

Applications:
- Text processing.
- Speech recognition.
- Time-series forecasting.

Limitations:
Traditional RNNs can struggle with long-term dependencies because of vanishing and exploding gradients.

23. LSTM AND GRU

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a type of recurrent neural network designed to learn long-term dependencies.

LSTM uses:
- Forget gate.
- Input gate.
- Output gate.
- Cell state.

Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is another recurrent architecture that uses update and reset gates to control information flow.

Both architectures help address limitations of traditional RNNs.

24. NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field of AI that enables computers to process, understand, and generate human language.

Common NLP tasks:
- Text classification.
- Sentiment analysis.
- Named Entity Recognition.
- Machine translation.
- Question answering.
- Text summarization.
- Language generation.

25. TEXT PREPROCESSING

Text preprocessing transforms raw text into a suitable format for analysis or model training.

Common operations:
- Lowercasing.
- Tokenization.
- Removing unwanted characters.
- Normalization.
- Stop-word removal where appropriate.
- Stemming.
- Lemmatization.

Tokenization:
Splits text into smaller units called tokens.

Stemming:
Reduces words to approximate root forms by removing prefixes or suffixes.

Lemmatization:
Converts words into dictionary base forms using linguistic information.

26. WORD EMBEDDINGS

Word embeddings represent words as numerical vectors in a continuous vector space.

Similar words may have vectors that are close to each other according to a similarity measure.

Examples of embedding approaches:
- Word2Vec.
- GloVe.
- FastText.

Embeddings help machine learning systems process semantic relationships between words.

27. TRANSFORMER ARCHITECTURE

A Transformer is a neural network architecture based on attention mechanisms rather than recurrence as its main sequence-processing mechanism.

Major components:
- Token embeddings.
- Positional information.
- Self-attention.
- Feed-forward networks.
- Residual connections.
- Layer normalization.

Transformers can process sequence positions in parallel during training, subject to attention-mask and architectural constraints.

28. SELF-ATTENTION MECHANISM

Self-attention allows each token to calculate how much attention it should give to other tokens in a sequence.

Three important vectors:
- Query (Q).
- Key (K).
- Value (V).

Scaled dot-product attention:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k))V

Where d_k is the dimension of the key vectors.

Attention helps the model capture relationships between different positions in a sequence.

29. MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION

Multi-head attention performs multiple attention operations in parallel.

Each attention head can learn different relationships between tokens.

The outputs of the heads are combined and projected to produce the final representation.

Benefits:
- Captures different types of relationships.
- Learns multiple contextual representations.
- Improves the model's ability to represent sequence information.

30. LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a neural network trained on large amounts of text to learn language patterns and predict tokens.

Many modern LLMs use Transformer architectures.

Typical training stages:
- Data collection and preparation.
- Tokenization.
- Pretraining.
- Instruction tuning or other adaptation.
- Evaluation.
- Deployment.

An autoregressive language model predicts the next token based on previous tokens.

31. TOKENIZATION IN LANGUAGE MODELS

Tokenization converts text into tokens that a language model can process.

Common approaches:
- Word-level tokenization.
- Character-level tokenization.
- Subword tokenization.

Subword tokenization divides text into frequently occurring smaller units and can represent many unseen words using known pieces.

Examples of subword methods include Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece.

Special tokens may represent:
- Beginning of sequence.
- End of sequence.
- Padding.
- Unknown tokens.
- Instruction or role boundaries.

32. AUTOREGRESSIVE TEXT GENERATION

Autoregressive generation produces text one token at a time.

Process:
1. Receive a prompt.
2. Convert the prompt into token IDs.
3. Pass the tokens through the model.
4. Obtain next-token scores or logits.
5. Convert scores into a probability distribution.
6. Select the next token.
7. Append the token to the sequence.
8. Repeat until a stopping condition is reached.

33. TEXT GENERATION PARAMETERS

Temperature:
Controls the randomness of token selection.

Lower temperature generally produces more predictable output. Higher temperature generally produces more varied output.

Top-K Sampling:
Restricts token selection to the K highest-scoring candidates.

Top-P Sampling:
Selects from the smallest group of candidates whose cumulative probability reaches a specified threshold.

Repetition Penalty:
Reduces the likelihood of repeatedly generating the same tokens.

Maximum New Tokens:
Limits how many tokens can be generated after the prompt.

34. RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines information retrieval with language generation.

Typical workflow:
1. Receive a user question.
2. Search a knowledge source for relevant information.
3. Retrieve the most relevant documents or passages.
4. Add retrieved information to the model's prompt.
5. Generate an answer grounded in the retrieved context.
6. Present source references when available.

Benefits:
- Uses external or updated information.
- Can answer questions about private documents.
- Reduces dependence on information stored only in model parameters.

RAG does not automatically guarantee factual accuracy. Retrieval quality and generated answers must be evaluated.

35. VECTOR DATABASES

A vector database stores and searches numerical vector representations.

Typical uses:
- Semantic search.
- Document retrieval.
- Recommendation systems.
- RAG applications.

Similarity measures:
- Cosine similarity.
- Euclidean distance.
- Dot product.

A vector database is one possible retrieval technology. A RAG system can also use keyword search, full-text search, or other retrieval methods.

Major tasks:
- Image classification.
- Object detection.
- Image segmentation.
- Face recognition.
- Optical Character Recognition.
- Image generation.

Image classification:
Assigns a category to an image.

Object detection:
Identifies objects and their locations, often using bounding boxes.

Image segmentation:
Assigns labels to individual pixels or image regions.

37. GENERATIVE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Generative AI refers to AI systems that generate new content based on patterns learned from training data.

Generated content can include:
- Text.
- Images.
- Audio.
- Video.
- Computer code.

Examples of model families:
- Autoregressive language models.
- Generative adversarial networks.
- Variational autoencoders.
- Diffusion models.

38. GENERATIVE ADVERSARIAL NETWORKS

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) consists of two neural networks:

Generator:
Creates synthetic samples.

Discriminator:
Attempts to distinguish real samples from generated samples.

The two networks are trained in competition. The generator learns to produce increasingly realistic samples.

Applications:
- Image generation.
- Image enhancement.
- Data augmentation.
- Synthetic data generation.

39. AI MODEL EVALUATION

Model evaluation measures how well an AI system performs on data not used for training.

Classification metrics:
- Accuracy.
- Precision.
- Recall.
- F1-score.
- Confusion matrix.

Regression metrics:
- Mean Absolute Error.
- Mean Squared Error.
- Root Mean Squared Error.
- R-squared.

Language model evaluation may examine:
- Perplexity.
- Next-token prediction accuracy.
- Instruction-following ability.
- Factual correctness.
- Relevance.
- Coherence.
- Safety.

40. OVERFITTING AND UNDERFITTING

Overfitting:
A model performs well on training data but poorly on unseen data because it has learned patterns that do not generalize.

Underfitting:
A model fails to learn important patterns even from its training data.

Methods to reduce overfitting:
- More representative training data.
- Data augmentation.
- Regularization.
- Dropout.
- Early stopping.
- Appropriate model complexity.

41. TRAINING, VALIDATION, AND TEST DATA

Training set:
Used to learn model parameters.

Validation set:
Used to tune hyperparameters and make model-development decisions.

Test set:
Used for final evaluation on data not used for training or tuning.

Separating these datasets helps estimate how well a model generalizes to unseen examples.

42. ETHICAL AND RESPONSIBLE AI

Important concerns:
- Bias and fairness.
- Privacy.
- Security.
- Explainability.
- Accountability.
- Reliability.
- Misuse.
- Environmental and computational cost.

AI systems can produce incorrect or biased outputs. Important decisions should include suitable human oversight.
