SCIENCE FUNDAMENTALS

1. INTRODUCTION TO SCIENCE

Science is the systematic study of the natural world through observation, experimentation, measurement, and reasoning.

Major branches:
- Physics: Studies matter, energy, motion, and forces.
- Chemistry: Studies matter, its composition, properties, and transformations.
- Biology: Studies living organisms and life processes.
- Earth Science: Studies the Earth and its systems.
- Astronomy: Studies celestial objects and the universe.

2. SCIENTIFIC METHOD

The scientific method is a systematic approach used to investigate questions and develop explanations.

Steps:
1. Observation: Identify a phenomenon.
2. Question: Formulate a question about the observation.
3. Hypothesis: Propose a testable explanation.
4. Experiment: Design and perform an investigation.
5. Data collection: Record observations and measurements.
6. Analysis: Interpret the collected data.
7. Conclusion: Determine whether the evidence supports the hypothesis.
8. Communication: Share findings for review and further investigation.

A scientific hypothesis must be testable and capable of being evaluated using evidence.

3. MEASUREMENT AND SI UNITS

Measurement assigns numerical values and units to physical quantities.

The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units:

Length: metre (m).
Mass: kilogram (kg).
Time: second (s).
Electric current: ampere (A).
Temperature: kelvin (K).
Amount of substance: mole (mol).
Luminous intensity: candela (cd).

Derived units:
- Velocity: metre per second (m/s).
- Acceleration: metre per second squared (m/s^2).
- Force: newton (N).
- Energy: joule (J).
- Power: watt (W).
- Pressure: pascal (Pa).

4. SCALARS AND VECTORS

A scalar quantity has magnitude only.

Examples:
- Mass.
- Time.
- Temperature.
- Energy.
- Distance.

A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction.

Examples:
- Displacement.
- Velocity.
- Acceleration.
- Force.
- Momentum.

5. MATTER

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Common states of matter:
- Solid: Has a definite shape and volume.
- Liquid: Has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container.
- Gas: Has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume.
- Plasma: An ionized state of matter containing charged particles.

Changes of state:
- Melting: Solid to liquid.
- Freezing: Liquid to solid.
- Vaporization: Liquid to gas.
- Condensation: Gas to liquid.
- Sublimation: Solid directly to gas.
- Deposition: Gas directly to solid.

6. MOTION

Motion is the change in position of an object with respect to a reference point over time.

Distance:
The total length of the path travelled. It is a scalar quantity.

Displacement:
The change in position from initial to final point. It is a vector quantity.

Speed:
Rate of change of distance.

Speed = Distance / Time.

Velocity:
Rate of change of displacement.

Average velocity = Displacement / Time taken.

Acceleration:
Rate of change of velocity.

Average acceleration = Change in velocity / Time taken.

7. TYPES OF MOTION

Linear motion:
Movement along a straight line.

Circular motion:
Movement along a circular path.

Rotational motion:
Movement of an object around an axis.

Oscillatory motion:
Repeated movement around an equilibrium position.

Periodic motion:
Motion that repeats after equal intervals of time.

8. NEWTON'S LAWS OF MOTION

First Law - Law of Inertia:
An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

Second Law:
The net force acting on an object equals the rate of change of its momentum.

For constant mass:

F = ma.

Where:
F = Net force.
m = Mass.
a = Acceleration.

Third Law:
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

The action and reaction forces act on different bodies.

9. FORCE

Force is an interaction that can change the motion or shape of an object.

SI unit: Newton (N).

Types:
- Gravitational force.
- Frictional force.
- Normal force.
- Tension.
- Magnetic force.
- Electrostatic force.

Balanced forces:
Net force is zero.

Unbalanced forces:
Net force is non-zero and can change an object's velocity.

10. FRICTION

Friction is a force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion between surfaces in contact.

Types:
- Static friction.
- Sliding friction.
- Rolling friction.
- Fluid friction.

Friction can:
- Help us walk.
- Allow vehicles to brake.
- Produce heat.
- Cause mechanical wear.

11. WORK, ENERGY, AND POWER

Work is done when a force causes displacement.

For a constant force:

W = F s cos(theta).

Where:
F = Force.
s = Displacement.
theta = Angle between force and displacement.

SI unit: Joule (J).

Energy is the capacity to do work.

Forms of energy:
- Kinetic energy.
- Potential energy.
- Thermal energy.
- Chemical energy.
- Electrical energy.
- Nuclear energy.
- Light energy.

Kinetic energy:

KE = 1/2 mv^2.

Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface:

PE = mgh.

Power is the rate of doing work.

Power = Work / Time.

SI unit: Watt (W).

12. LAW OF CONSERVATION OF ENERGY

Energy cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system. It can only be transferred or transformed from one form to another.

Example:
In a falling object, gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases, ignoring energy losses.

13. MOMENTUM

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity.

p = mv.

SI unit: kg m/s.

Momentum is a vector quantity.

Impulse:
Impulse equals the change in momentum.

J = F * delta(t), for constant force.

In an isolated system, total momentum remains constant if the net external force is zero.

14. GRAVITATION

Gravitation is the attractive force between objects with mass.

Newton's law of universal gravitation:

F = G(m1*m2)/r^2.

Where:
G = Universal gravitational constant.
m1 and m2 = Masses of objects.
r = Distance between their centres.

Acceleration due to gravity near Earth's surface is approximately 9.8 m/s^2.

15. PRESSURE

Pressure is force acting per unit area.

P = F/A.

SI unit: Pascal (Pa).

Pressure in a fluid at rest increases with depth.

For a fluid of density rho at depth h:

P = rho*g*h.

This is gauge pressure due to the fluid column.

16. HEAT AND TEMPERATURE

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Temperature measures the thermal state of a system.

Heat transfer methods:

Conduction:
Transfer of thermal energy through direct microscopic interactions, commonly important in solids.

Convection:
Transfer of thermal energy through the bulk movement of fluids.

Radiation:
Transfer of energy through electromagnetic waves and does not require a material medium.

17. SPECIFIC HEAT CAPACITY

Specific heat capacity is the energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin.

Q = mc(delta T).

Where:
Q = Heat transferred.
m = Mass.
c = Specific heat capacity.
delta T = Change in temperature.

SI unit of specific heat capacity: J/(kg K).

18. THERMODYNAMICS

Thermodynamics studies heat, work, temperature, and energy transformations.

Zeroth Law:
If two systems are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

First Law:
Energy is conserved.

Using the convention that W is work done by the system:

delta U = Q - W.

Second Law:
The entropy of an isolated system does not decrease in a spontaneous process.

Third Law:
The entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as its temperature approaches absolute zero.

19. WAVES

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one location to another.

Mechanical waves require a material medium.

Electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum.

Types:
- Transverse waves.
- Longitudinal waves.

Transverse wave:
Particle oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.

Longitudinal wave:
Particle oscillations are parallel to the direction of wave propagation.

20. WAVE PARAMETERS

Wavelength:
Distance between two successive points in the same phase, such as consecutive crests.

Frequency:
Number of complete cycles per second.

SI unit: Hertz (Hz).

Time period:
Time required for one complete cycle.

T = 1/f.

Wave speed:

v = f(lambda).

Where lambda represents wavelength.

Amplitude:
Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.

21. SOUND

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating objects.

In air, sound generally travels as a longitudinal wave.

Properties:
- Frequency determines perceived pitch.
- Amplitude is related to sound intensity.
- Timbre helps distinguish sound sources.

Sound requires a material medium and cannot travel through a vacuum.

The speed of sound depends on the properties and conditions of the medium.

22. LIGHT

Light is electromagnetic radiation visible to the human eye.

Properties:
- Travels through a vacuum.
- Undergoes reflection and refraction.
- Exhibits diffraction and interference.
- Can be polarized.

Speed of light in vacuum:

c approximately equals 3 * 10^8 m/s.

23. REFLECTION OF LIGHT

Reflection occurs when light returns into the original medium after striking a surface.

Laws:
1. The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in the same plane.
2. Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.

Applications:
- Mirrors.
- Periscopes.
- Optical instruments.

24. REFRACTION OF LIGHT

Refraction is the change in direction of light when it passes between media with different refractive indices.

Snell's law:

n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2).

Refractive index:

n = c/v.

Where:
c = Speed of light in vacuum.
v = Speed of light in the medium.

Applications:
- Lenses.
- Spectacles.
- Cameras.
- Microscopes.

25. ELECTRICITY

Electricity involves electric charge and its effects.

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter.

Two types:
- Positive charge.
- Negative charge.

Like charges repel.
Unlike charges attract.

SI unit of electric charge: Coulomb (C).

26. ELECTRIC CURRENT

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.

I = Q/t.

SI unit: Ampere (A).

Conventional current direction is defined as the direction positive charges would move.

27. VOLTAGE AND RESISTANCE

Voltage is the electric potential difference between two points.

SI unit: Volt (V).

Resistance is the opposition to electric current.

SI unit: Ohm (ohm symbol).

28. OHM'S LAW

Ohm's law states that for a conductor under constant physical conditions, current is directly proportional to voltage.

V = IR.

Where:
V = Voltage.
I = Current.
R = Resistance.

29. ELECTRIC POWER AND ENERGY

Electric power is the rate of electrical energy transfer.

P = VI.

For a resistive element:

P = I^2 R.

Also:

P = V^2/R.

Electrical energy:

E = Pt.

Commercial electrical energy is commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

30. ELECTRIC CIRCUITS

A basic electric circuit contains:
- Source.
- Conducting wires.
- Load.
- Switching or control element.

Series circuit:
Components are connected along a single current path.

Parallel circuit:
Components are connected across common nodes, providing multiple current paths.

For resistors in series:

R_total = R1 + R2 + ... + Rn.

For resistors in parallel:

1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... + 1/Rn.

31. MAGNETISM

Magnetism is associated with moving electric charges and magnetic moments.

A magnet has north and south poles.

Like magnetic poles repel.
Unlike magnetic poles attract.

Magnetic fields describe the magnetic influence in a region.

SI unit of magnetic flux density: Tesla (T).

32. ELECTROMAGNETISM

Electromagnetism studies the relationship between electric and magnetic fields.

An electric current can produce a magnetic field.

A changing magnetic flux can induce an electromotive force.

Faraday's law describes electromagnetic induction.

Lenz's law states that the induced current produces an effect that opposes the change in magnetic flux that caused it.

Applications:
- Electric motors.
- Generators.
- Transformers.
- Electromagnets.

33. ATOMIC STRUCTURE

An atom is the basic unit of a chemical element that retains its chemical identity.

Main subatomic particles:

Proton:
Positive charge; located in the nucleus.

Neutron:
No electric charge; located in the nucleus.

Electron:
Negative charge; occupies regions around the nucleus.

Atomic number:
Number of protons in an atom's nucleus.

Mass number:
Total number of protons and neutrons.

In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

34. ISOTOPES AND IONS

Isotopes:
Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Ions:
Electrically charged atoms or groups of atoms.

Cation:
A positively charged ion formed by losing electrons.

Anion:
A negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons.

35. PERIODIC TABLE

The periodic table organizes chemical elements according to increasing atomic number.

Rows are called periods.

Columns are called groups.

Elements in the same group often have similar chemical properties because of related valence electron configurations.

Broad categories:
- Metals.
- Non-metals.
- Metalloids.

36. CHEMICAL BONDING

Chemical bonds hold atoms or ions together.

Ionic bond:
Forms through electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, commonly following electron transfer.

Covalent bond:
Forms when atoms share electron pairs.

Metallic bond:
Involves attraction between positive metal ion cores and delocalized electrons.

37. CHEMICAL REACTIONS

A chemical reaction converts reactants into products through the rearrangement of atoms.

Reactants:
Starting substances.

Products:
Substances formed.

Common types:
- Combination reaction.
- Decomposition reaction.
- Displacement reaction.
- Double displacement reaction.
- Combustion reaction.
- Redox reaction.

A balanced chemical equation has equal numbers of each type of atom on both sides.

38. ACIDS, BASES, AND SALTS

Acid:
A substance that donates protons in the Brønsted-Lowry definition.

Base:
A substance that accepts protons in the Brønsted-Lowry definition.

Salt:
An ionic compound formed through reactions involving acids and bases or other chemical processes.

pH:
A measure related to hydrogen ion activity.

At approximately 25 degrees Celsius in dilute aqueous solutions:
- pH below 7 is acidic.
- pH equal to 7 is neutral.
- pH above 7 is basic.

39. CHEMICAL STOICHIOMETRY

Stoichiometry studies quantitative relationships between reactants and products in chemical reactions.

A mole represents a specified amount of substance.

Avogadro constant:

6.02214076 * 10^23 entities per mole.

Molar mass:
Mass of one mole of a substance.

Balanced chemical equations provide mole ratios for calculations.

40. INTRODUCTION TO BIOLOGY

Biology is the scientific study of living organisms and life processes.

Major branches:
- Botany.
- Zoology.
- Microbiology.
- Genetics.
- Ecology.
- Molecular biology.
- Biotechnology.

Characteristics commonly associated with living organisms include cellular organization, metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, reproduction, and evolution across populations.

41. CELL THEORY

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Cell theory:
1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function.
3. New cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Two broad cell types:
- Prokaryotic cells.
- Eukaryotic cells.

42. PROKARYOTIC AND EUKARYOTIC CELLS

Prokaryotic cells:
Do not have a membrane-bound nucleus. Their genetic material is located in a nucleoid region.

Examples include bacteria and archaea.

Eukaryotic cells:
Have a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Examples include animal, plant, fungal, and protist cells.

43. CELL ORGANELLES

Nucleus:
Contains most of the cell's genetic material and regulates gene expression.

Mitochondria:
Carry out important stages of cellular respiration and produce much of the cell's ATP.

Ribosomes:
Synthesize proteins.

Endoplasmic reticulum:
Involved in protein and lipid synthesis and processing.

Golgi apparatus:
Modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids.

Lysosomes:
Contain enzymes that help break down cellular materials.

Chloroplasts:
In plants and algae, carry out photosynthesis.

Cell membrane:
Controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.

44. DNA AND RNA

DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid):
Stores hereditary information in most organisms.

RNA (Ribonucleic Acid):
Participates in gene expression and protein synthesis and performs other cellular roles.

DNA generally contains:
- Adenine.
- Thymine.
- Guanine.
- Cytosine.

RNA generally contains:
- Adenine.
- Uracil.
- Guanine.
- Cytosine.

45. GENETICS

Genetics is the study of heredity and variation.

Gene:
A segment of genetic material associated with a functional product.

Allele:
An alternative form of a gene.

Genotype:
The genetic constitution of an organism for a particular trait or set of traits.

Phenotype:
Observable characteristics resulting from genetic and environmental influences.

Dominant allele:
An allele that can determine a phenotype in a heterozygote under a simple dominance model.

Recessive allele:
An allele whose associated phenotype is masked in a heterozygote under a simple dominance model.

46. MENDEL'S LAWS OF INHERITANCE

Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants helped establish fundamental principles of inheritance.

Law of Segregation:
The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives one allele.

Law of Independent Assortment:
Alleles of different genes assort independently when the genes are unlinked or sufficiently far apart, subject to genetic linkage.

Law of Dominance:
In simple complete-dominance cases, one allele can mask the expression of another in a heterozygote.

47. PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy.

In oxygenic photosynthesis, carbon dioxide and water are used to produce carbohydrates, and oxygen is released.

Simplified equation:

6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6O2.

In plants, photosynthesis mainly occurs in chloroplasts.

48. CELLULAR RESPIRATION

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells extract energy from nutrients and use it to produce ATP.

Aerobic respiration uses oxygen as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain.

A simplified overall equation for glucose oxidation is:

C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy.

Major stages:
- Glycolysis.
- Pyruvate oxidation.
- Citric acid cycle.
- Oxidative phosphorylation.

49. HUMAN BODY SYSTEMS

Digestive system:
Breaks down food and absorbs nutrients.

Respiratory system:
Supports gas exchange, taking in oxygen and removing carbon dioxide.

Circulatory system:
Transports blood, oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.

Nervous system:
Coordinates rapid communication and responses using electrical and chemical signals.

Skeletal system:
Supports the body, protects organs, and helps movement.

Muscular system:
Produces movement and helps maintain posture.

Excretory system:
Removes metabolic waste and regulates water and electrolyte balance.

Endocrine system:
Uses hormones to regulate body functions.

Immune system:
Defends the body against pathogens and other harmful substances.

50. ECOLOGY

Ecology studies interactions between organisms and their environment.

Levels of ecological organization:
- Organism.
- Population.
- Community.
- Ecosystem.
- Biome.
- Biosphere.

Ecosystem:
A system consisting of living organisms and their physical environment interacting together.

Biotic factors:
Living components.

Abiotic factors:
Non-living environmental components such as water, light, soil, and temperature.

51. FOOD CHAINS AND FOOD WEBS

Food chain:
A linear sequence showing the transfer of energy and nutrients between organisms.

Producer:
An organism that produces organic matter, commonly through photosynthesis.

Consumer:
An organism that obtains energy by consuming other organisms or organic matter.

Decomposer:
An organism that breaks down dead organic matter and recycles nutrients.

Food web:
A network of interconnected food chains.

Energy generally decreases at successive trophic levels because organisms use energy for life processes and lose much of it as heat.

52. BIODIVERSITY

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels.

Importance:
- Maintains ecosystem functioning.
- Provides food and resources.
- Supports pollination and nutrient cycling.
- Contributes to ecosystem resilience.
- Has cultural, scientific, and economic value.

Threats:
- Habitat destruction.
- Pollution.
- Climate change.
- Invasive species.
- Overexploitation.

53. ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE

Environmental science studies interactions between natural systems and human activities.

Major environmental issues:
- Climate change.
- Air pollution.
- Water pollution.
- Soil degradation.
- Deforestation.
- Biodiversity loss.
- Waste management.

Sustainable development aims to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

54. CLIMATE CHANGE

Climate change refers to long-term changes in temperature and weather patterns.

Human activities, especially greenhouse gas emissions from burning fossil fuels and land-use changes, are major drivers of recent global warming.

Important greenhouse gases:
- Carbon dioxide.
- Methane.
- Nitrous oxide.
- Certain industrial gases.

Potential effects:
- Rising average temperatures.
- Sea-level rise.
- Changes in rainfall patterns.
- More frequent or intense heat extremes in many regions.
- Ecosystem disruption.

55. SCIENCE QUESTION AND ANSWER EXAMPLES

Q: What is science?
A: Science is the systematic study of the natural world through observation, experimentation, measurement, and reasoning.

Q: What is the scientific method?
A: The scientific method is a structured process involving observation, hypothesis formation, experimentation, analysis, and conclusion.

Q: What is matter?
A: Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Q: What is Newton's first law?
A: An object remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless acted upon by a net external force.

Q: What is Newton's second law?
A: The net force equals the rate of change of momentum; for constant mass, F = ma.

Q: What is energy?
A: Energy is a physical quantity associated with the capacity to cause change or perform work.

Q: What is the law of conservation of energy?
A: Energy cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system; it can only be transferred or transformed.

Q: What is heat?
A: Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Q: What is the difference between heat and temperature?
A: Heat is energy in transfer, whereas temperature describes the thermal state of a system.

Q: What is a wave?
A: A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one location to another.

Q: What is electric current?
A: Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.

Q: State Ohm's law.
A: For a conductor under constant physical conditions, voltage is equal to current multiplied by resistance: V = IR.

Q: What is an atom?
A: An atom is the basic unit of a chemical element that retains its chemical identity.

Q: What is an isotope?
A: Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.

Q: What is a chemical bond?
A: A chemical bond is an interaction that holds atoms or ions together.

Q: What is biology?
A: Biology is the scientific study of living organisms and life processes.

Q: What is a cell?
A: A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

Q: What is DNA?
A: DNA is the molecule that stores hereditary information in most organisms.

Q: What is photosynthesis?
A: Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy and, in oxygenic photosynthesis, releases oxygen.

Q: What is cellular respiration?
A: Cellular respiration is the process through which cells extract energy from nutrients and produce ATP.

Q: What is an ecosystem?
A: An ecosystem includes living organisms and their physical environment interacting as a system.

Q: What is biodiversity?
A: Biodiversity is the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels.

Q: What is climate change?
A: Climate change refers to long-term changes in climate patterns, including the recent warming driven largely by human greenhouse gas emissions.
